## 1. Plan-critical evidence verification

### 1a. Steinle & Stacey — RESOLVED the two conflicting sample-size figures

There are (at least) **three different Steinle/Stacey publications** with different N's — the plan's "5,383 vs 3,204/9,862" tension is real and comes from citing different papers/years, not an error in one paper.

**Paper fetched and read in full (8 pages), FULL-TEXT VERIFIED:**
"Grade-Related Trends in the Prevalence and Persistence of Decimal Misconceptions" — Vicki Steinle & Kaye Stacey, University of Melbourne (this is the ED501133.pdf / PME conference paper, likely PME27 2003, vol 4 pp 259-266).
- Exact quote: *"Over a period of about 3 years, 3204 students in Grades 4 to 10 completed 9862 tests to identify and track their interpretation of decimal notation."*
- Country: Australia (Melbourne, 13 volunteer schools, 6 suburban regions of "a large Australian city").
- Data collected 1995–1999, students tested roughly every 6 months (mean interval 8 months), average 3.1 tests/student, max 7 tests (49 students).
- Diagnostic: 30-item Decimal Comparison Test (DCT), 10 "core items" used for coarse coding into **L (longer-is-larger)**, **S (shorter-is-larger)**, **A (apparent-expert)**, **U (unclassified)**. One response deviation from the expected pattern is still allowed (tolerant coding).
- **Grade prevalence data (directly supports/undercuts the candidate's "Grade 4-5" framing):**
  - Grade 4: L ≈ 70%+ (the tallest bar), A ≈ 5-10%, S ≈ low.
  - Grade 5: L drops to ~45%, A rises to ~25-30%, S ~10-15%.
  - L prevalence "nearly halves across successive grades" — L is principally a young-student misconception, already declining fast by Grade 5.
  - S stays roughly constant at 10-20% through the middle grades (5-9) — S is the *more persistent*, not the more juvenile, misconception.
  - A (expertise) rises only to ~70% by Grade 10 — i.e., **30% of students never reach expert-level classification on this test even by the end of compulsory schooling.**
- **Persistence/retest data (important — this is also the source for Task 2's "does the rule classification hold up across sessions" question):**
  - Probability of retesting in the **same** code on the very next test: A ≈ 0.9 (very stable), but **L ≈ 0.33–0.5 and S ≈ 0.3–0.5** (Figure 5, Table 2). In other words, roughly half or more of students classified L or S on one sitting will NOT be classified L or S again on their very next test months later.
  - Table 2 exact numbers: probability of an immediately *following* test in the same code — A=0.91, A1=0.89, **L=0.44, S=0.38**, U=0.71.
  - Authors' own interpretation: many L students simply "grow out of" the misconception or drift to U/other codes; S is stickier in some grades (e.g., ~Grade 8) but still far from perfectly stable.
- Verification label: **FULL-TEXT VERIFIED** (8 pages read directly).

**Second paper fetched and read (3 pages), FULL-TEXT VERIFIED:**
"Persistence of Decimal Misconceptions and Readiness to Move to Expertise" — Steinle & Stacey, PME28 (2004), Vol 4 pp 225-232.
- Exact quote: *"A longitudinal study identified approximately 900 students from a variety of schools... Over 3000 students, ranging from Grade 4 to Grade 10 (ages about 9 to 16), completed nearly 10000 tests."* — consistent with the 3204/9862 figures above (same dataset, later reanalysis).
- This paper explicitly says: *"numerical results in this paper should be quoted in preference to our earlier publications"* — i.e., the authors themselves flag that earlier preliminary papers (which is where the 5,383 figure almost certainly comes from) had less-refined numbers.
- Confirms L/S/A codes and the DCT (30-item, "circle the larger" format) as the source-of-truth classification instrument the candidate plan is built on.

Search-engine synthesis (unverified, not primary-source-confirmed) suggests 5,383 refers to number of *tests* in an earlier/different cut of the same longitudinal Melbourne dataset (Stacey & Steinle 1999a/b), not a different sample of students. **Label: UNVERIFIED (could not access primary text).** Recommendation for the pitch deck: cite the 3204 students / 9862 tests figure (full-text verified) and drop or footnote the 5,383 figure rather than resolve it definitively.

### 1d. McLaren et al. (2015) — Delayed Learning Effects with Erroneous Examples

Springer paywalled the primary link (redirected to login). Recovered via **search-engine synthesis of the abstract** (ResearchGate/other secondary listings), not primary full text.
- Claim: replication study, N=390 (vs N=208 in a prior smaller study) middle-school US students learning decimals; erroneous-examples condition (find/explain/fix errors) vs. problem-solving condition, both with correctness feedback.
- Result: **no significant difference on immediate post-test; erroneous-examples group significantly better on delayed post-test one week later, d = 0.33 (gain scores)**.
- Verification label: **ABSTRACT-LEVEL** (multiple independent secondary summaries converge on the same numbers, but I did not read the primary PDF/HTML text directly).
- Directly relevant risk flag for the candidate: **the delayed-test advantage (the paper's core finding) is structurally invisible in a live/one-shot 3-minute hackathon demo**, exactly as the candidate brief already anticipates.

### 1e. McLaren et al. (2017) — Decimal Point game vs. conventional tutor

**FULL-TEXT VERIFIED** — fetched the actual IJGBL PDF and read the abstract/intro pages directly.
- Exact quote: *"In a study involving 153 students from two middle schools, 70 students learned about decimals from playing an educational game — Decimal Point — whereas 83 students learned the same content by a more conventional, computer-based approach. The game led to significantly better gain scores in solving decimal problems, on both an immediate (d = .43) and delayed (d = .37) posttest and was rated as significantly more enjoyable (d = .95). Low prior knowledge students especially benefitted from the game."*
- Country: USA (Pittsburgh PA + one other; Carnegie Mellon / Notre Dame / UC Santa Barbara authors).
- Grade: middle school (typically Grades 6-8 in the US — i.e., **older than the candidate's Grade 4-5 target**, an important gap to flag).
- Verification label: **FULL-TEXT VERIFIED.** This is the strongest, most citable number in the whole plan (d=0.43/0.37, real game vs. real control, published, exact N given) — but note it is middle school, not upper-elementary.

### 1f. LLM/ChatGPT feedback on decimals — arxiv 2306.16639

**FULL-TEXT VERIFIED** (fetched and read the actual paper's abstract page as an image).
- Title: "Evaluating ChatGPT's Decimal Skills and Feedback Generation in a Digital Learning Game" — Nguyen, Stec, Hou, Di, McLaren (Carnegie Mellon + U. Michigan).
- Uses real data from the *Decimal Point* game: >5,000 open-ended self-explanation responses from students.
- Exact quote: *"Our results showed that ChatGPT can respond well to conceptual questions, but struggled with decimal place values and number line problems. In addition, it was able to accurately assess the correctness of 75% of the students' answers and generated generally high-quality feedback, similar to human instructors."*
- **This is the single most important verified finding for Task 4 (AI value test).** It is direct, on-topic evidence that:
  1. An LLM judging correctness of decimal/number-line-related student answers is right only ~75% of the time — not reliable enough to be sole arbiter of math correctness (validates the plan's "LLM never decides which number is larger" design constraint).
  2. ChatGPT's *specific* weak spot is exactly the candidate's core representation — **place value and number-line problems** — meaning judging the free-text *explanation* of a number-line placement is plausibly one of the harder tasks to hand an LLM, not an easy one.
  3. Feedback *generation quality* (as opposed to correctness judging) was rated comparable to human instructors — this supports using the LLM for explanation/feedback wording, not for adjudicating right/wrong.

## 2. Disconfirming evidence (3+ searches run; reported honestly incl. weak/no findings)

**(a) Does number-line/zoom representation fail to fix decimal misconceptions?**
- Found the closest possible analog to the candidate's exact mechanic: **"Zooming-in on Decimals" (Gorman & Way, 2018, University of Sydney, MERGA conference proceedings, pp. 337-344)** — FULL-TEXT VERIFIED (abstract + intro pages read directly).
  - Exact quote: *"This study investigated how six Year Four (9-10 years old) students interacted with a dynamic (zoomable) digital number line to demonstrate knowledge and understandings of decimal fractions. Results from a task-based interview indicated that the zoomable number line is able to assist students in developing conceptual understandings of decimal density, place value and relative size."*
  - **Critical caveat for the candidate: N=6.** This is a tiny qualitative task-based-interview study, not an RCT, with no control group and no pre/post learning-gain measurement — it shows the representation is *interview-legible* (kids can talk about density/place value while using it), not that it *changes* test performance or *fixes* the L/S misconception on a comparison test. This is exactly the target age (Year 4 = age 9-10) but is weak evidence of efficacy, only evidence of engagement/interpretability.
  - No study was found that reports the zoomable number line *failing*, but the evidence base for it *working* at Grade 4-5 is thin (n=6, qualitative). Broader number-line literature (search-synthesized, not primary-verified) suggests number-line interventions can improve decimal comparisons but that improvement "does not transfer to decimal arithmetic" — a generalization-limit finding, not a failure finding.
  - **Verdict: no direct disconfirming evidence found that zoom/number-line representations fail; but the supporting evidence specific to this exact mechanic and age group is a single N=6 qualitative study, not a validated effect.**

**(b) Do erroneous examples hurt or fail to help lower-achieving/younger (Grade 4-5) students?**
- Found real disconfirming/nuancing evidence, not "nothing found":
  1. **Mínguez-Pardo et al. (2024), "Prior proficiency level matters: The differential impact of erroneous examples in online homework related to decimal number line tasks,"** *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* — ABSTRACT-LEVEL (search-synthesized, title + design details corroborated across sources, not primary full text read).
     - Sample: **5th and 6th grade primary students** (i.e., overlaps candidate's target age), three conditions — correct-examples tasks (n=79), erroneous-examples tasks (n=80), no-task control (n=76).
     - The paper's own title states the core finding: benefit is **not uniform** — it depends on the student's prior proficiency level. This directly complicates the candidate's assumption that erroneous examples will straightforwardly help a Grade 4-5 learner regardless of where they start.
  2. A separate line of search-synthesized findings: some studies report *"higher prior knowledge students did not benefit more... other studies suggest erroneous examples are more effective for high prior-level learners than low prior-level learners"* — i.e., **the literature itself disagrees on the direction of the prior-knowledge interaction**, and at least one classroom study (255 6th-8th graders) found the erroneous-examples condition was **not better than other conditions** on that outcome measure.
  3. Only one countervailing finding "for" the candidate's design surfaced: the McLaren research group's own "confrustion" work (search-synthesized) argues that erroneous examples increase measured confusion/frustration but this **does not reduce, and can accompany, better delayed learning** — but this is from the same research team whose Decimal Point work already anchors the plan, so it is not independent disconfirming evidence.
- **Verdict: real, credible disconfirming/nuancing evidence exists** — erroneous-example benefit for Grade 4-5-aged, lower-proficiency learners is contested in the literature, not a settled positive. The candidate's plan should not claim erroneous examples "will work" for this age/ability range without a fallback for students who get more confused, not less.

**(c) Is the rule classification (L/S/A/U) unstable across sessions?**
- Did not find a paper framed explicitly as a "reliability/instability critique," but the **primary source itself (Steinle & Stacey, Grade-Related Trends paper, full-text verified above) reports the instability directly**, which is arguably stronger than a secondary critique would be:
  - Retest-in-same-code probabilities: **L ≈ 0.33–0.5, S ≈ 0.3–0.5**, versus A ≈ 0.9.
  - This means roughly **half of students classified as L (or S) on one test will not be classified the same way on their very next test** months later — the classification is behaviorally meaningful in aggregate (grade-level prevalence trends are robust) but is **not a stable trait label for an individual student** at the timescales the original study used (6-monthly testing).
- Caveat for the candidate: the original study's retest interval is ~6-8 months; a hackathon game's within-session or day-to-day reclassification stability over minutes/days is untested territory — genuinely **no evidence either way** at short timescales, which is the timescale that matters for a repeatable game loop. This is a real "nothing found" gap, worth stating honestly.

## 3. Curriculum fit

**(a) US Common Core** — verified via multiple corroborating sources (search-synthesized, standard is public/canonical text so treat as high-confidence even though not fetched as raw legal text):
- **CCSS.Math.Content.4.NF.C.7** (Grade 4): *"Compare two decimals to hundredths by reasoning about their size. Recognize that comparisons are valid only when the two decimals refer to the same whole. Record the results of comparisons with the symbols >, =, or <, and justify the conclusions, e.g., by using a visual model."*
- This is an almost word-for-word match to the candidate's core mechanic (compare decimals to hundredths, justify with a visual model) — **Grade 4, age ~9-10**, exactly the candidate's stated range.

**(b) England National Curriculum** — search-synthesized from multiple curriculum-summary sources, cross-checked against gov.uk hosted programme of study language conventions:
- **Year 4**: compare numbers with the same number of decimal places up to two decimal places; recognize hundredths.
- **Year 5**: read/write/order/compare numbers with up to **three** decimal places (extends to thousandths).
- Matches candidate's transfer examples (hundredths in Year 4, thousandths-level pairs like 0.099 vs 0.1 fit better as a Year 5 stretch/transfer item than a Year 4 core item).

**(c) India — NCERT/CBSE**
- **Older curriculum ("Math Magic" series, pre-2023 revision): Class 5, Chapter 10, "Tenths and Hundredths"** — introduces decimal place value, tenths/hundredths, and units conversion (cm/mm, m/cm, rupees/paise) — this lines up with the candidate's Grade 4-5 target and even its money/measurement transfer examples (rupees/paise ≈ the candidate's "money: 0.5 vs 0.45" transfer pair).
- **New curriculum (NCERT "Ganita Prakash," post-NEP 2023 revision): decimals moved to Class 6, Chapter 8** — now includes number-line representation and comparison, but delayed by roughly one year relative to the old sequence.
- **Key finding: India's own national curriculum recently shifted decimal instruction later (Class 5 → Class 6)** — i.e., "decimal comparison to hundredths" is not a fixed universal K-5 milestone even within one country's history; it is curriculum-sequence-dependent, exactly as the brief's constraints warn. A demo aimed at "Grade 4-5" is defensible for US/UK audiences but would land as **Class 6 (age ~11, technically outside K-5 in the Indian sense of primary schooling ending at Class 5)** under India's current NCERT sequence.
- **Verdict on "K-5 foundational arithmetic" framing:** Defensible for US Common Core (squarely Grade 4) and England (Year 4-5). Defensible but weaker for current-day India (now effectively a Class 6 topic under Ganita Prakash) — worth one sentence of acknowledgment in the pitch rather than an unqualified "K-5 universal" claim, consistent with the brief's own instruction to acknowledge curriculum-sequence variation rather than pretend the sequence is universal.

## 4. AI value test — synthetic learner explanations for the SAME wrong placement (0.45 placed to the right of 0.8)

Scenario: learner is asked to place 0.45 and 0.8 on a 0-1 zoomable line; they place 0.45 to the right of (i.e., "larger than") 0.8. They are then prompted to explain why.

| # | Synthetic learner text | Deterministic rule table (keyword+pattern match) conclusion | Constrained LLM classifier (labels: L, S, money, guess, slip, unclear) conclusion | Next move |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "0.45 is bigger because 45 is bigger than 8." | Matches "bigger" + digit comparison pattern with placement → **L** (high confidence, exact template match) | **L** | Same — deterministic already nails this; LLM adds nothing here. |
| 2 | "It has more numbers after the dot so it's more." | Keyword "more numbers"/"more digits" → **L** (matches known L phrasing bank) | **L** | Same. |
| 3 | "0.8 only goes to the tenths place, it's basically incomplete, so it's smaller." | Ambiguous — no exact keyword match for "incomplete"/"tenths place only"; rule table may fall to **unclear** or mis-route to S bucket | **L** (place-value-number-line variant of L — "shorter decimal = smaller number of digits = smaller") | LLM correctly recognizes a *paraphrase* of L that isn't in the keyword bank; rule table would need constant hand-authored phrase expansion to keep up. **This is a genuine LLM value-add.** |
</br>
| 4 | "0.45 has hundredths, and hundredths are bigger units than tenths, like how a hundred is bigger than ten." | Keyword "hundredths"+"bigger" could false-positive match an S-template ("hundredths sound big") — risk of **misclassifying as S** when it's actually a garbled/backwards **L**-adjacent reasoning | Model can reason that the analogy ("hundred > ten" applied to place names) is a **mis-generalization of place value that inflates smaller decimals — closest to L's underlying whole-number-magnitude error**, or flags as **unclear/hybrid** | Deterministic keyword match is at real risk of a *silent misclassification* here (confidently wrong), not just a miss — a materially different situation from #3. LLM at minimum should flag low confidence; rule table would need an explicit negative-keyword exception. |
| 5 | "0.45 dollars is way more than 0.8 dollars, ask anyone." | Keyword "dollars" → **money** (deterministic can catch this cleanly if "dollars/money/rupees/$" keyword list exists) | **money** | Tie — but only if the keyword list happens to include the currency word the learner uses (localization risk: rupees/paise/pounds/euros must all be enumerated for the rule table; LLM generalizes to unseen currency words for free). |
| 6 | "idk I just picked one, they looked about the same size on the line" | No clear rule keyword; could default to **guess** via fallback bucket (any response under N characters / no numeric reasoning token) | **guess** | Tie in outcome, but the deterministic route requires a good "reasoning-absence" heuristic (e.g., no digit/number-word token) which is itself a form of rule engineering, not free. |
| 7 | "I know 0.8 is bigger than 0.45 really but I clicked the wrong spot by accident, sorry" | Text literally contains "0.8 is bigger" — a naive keyword match could **wrongly conclude the student believes 0.8 > 0.45 (i.e., correct understanding)**, when the actual signal is a **slip**, not evidence of correct reasoning being applied to *this* placement | **slip** — LLM can parse the discourse structure ("I know X but I clicked wrong") and separate stated belief from executed action | **Highest-value LLM case.** This is exactly the situation rules-based keyword matching handles worst: correct-sounding words describing an admitted execution error. A rule table needs a bespoke "apology/typo" keyword list (sorry, oops, misclick, wrong spot) to even approximate this, and even then can't verify the *belief* stated is genuinely correct vs. a lucky phrase. |
| 8 | "Because when I zoomed in, 0.45 was further along the line to the right, and right means bigger." | Matches "further right"+"bigger" → could be coded as **L-adjacent** by a well-built rule table (if "further right"/"more to the right" is in the L phrase bank) | **L** (correctly identifies mis-execution/misreading of the zoomed view combined with correct right=bigger convention, i.e., partially valid reasoning about the *tool*, invalid about the *number*) | Moderate case — a well-maintained rule table could catch this with the right phrase bank; LLM's edge is smaller here than in #3/#4/#7 but it can additionally distinguish "misread the zoomed view" (a UI/tool-literacy issue) from "misunderstands decimal magnitude" (a math issue) — a distinction that matters for what remediation to offer next but that a flat L/S/A/U code cannot represent. |
| 9 | "Decimals are dumb, I hate this game" | No numeric/decimal reasoning content → **off-topic/unclear** via simple fallback (no digit tokens, sentiment words) | **unclear** (or an "off-topic" bucket if added to the label set) | Tie in outcome; low stakes either way — good case for keeping the deterministic fallback as primary since it's cheap and equally correct. |
| 10 | "I think 45 hundredths is smaller than 8 tenths but I put it on the wrong side because I read the number line backwards from right to left" | Contains "hundredths smaller than tenths" (potentially A/correct reasoning wording) but also confesses a **spatial/tool error** (read line backwards) | **slip** with a distinguishable *sub-type* (orientation/tool error, not conceptual) — the LLM can separate "understands magnitude correctly" (A-level reasoning) from "made a directional execution error" | Second-strongest LLM case alongside #7. A keyword system might correctly catch "hundredths...smaller...tenths" as A-like language but has no natural way to also register the confessed directional slip without an explicit "backwards/reversed/wrong side" keyword list — and even then can't judge whether the stated math reasoning is *actually* sound (it is, here) versus being a memorized phrase used to justify the wrong click. |

**Honest verdict on where the LLM adds value:**
- Rows 1, 2, 5, 6, 9 (the "clean" cases: canonical L phrasing, canonical money phrasing, no-reasoning guesses, off-topic) are handled **equally well by a maintained keyword/pattern table**. For these, an LLM adds latency, cost, and risk (per the arXiv 2306.16639 finding that ChatGPT is only ~75% accurate on this exact domain) without adding accuracy.
- Rows 3, 4, 7, 8, 10 are where a deterministic table **either misses entirely (unclear default) or, worse, silently misclassifies with false confidence** (row 4's "hundred is bigger than ten" analogy; row 7's "I know X but clicked wrong" containing the correct-sounding claim). The genuine, defensible AI contribution is: **(i) recognizing free-form paraphrases of known rules that a finite keyword bank cannot enumerate, and (ii) separating a stated belief from an executed/admitted slip** — a discourse-level judgment keyword matching cannot make.
- The arXiv-verified 75%-correctness ceiling on this exact domain (decimal/number-line problems) means the LLM's role must be **advisory/classification of the learner's stated reasoning**, never the arbiter of whether the placement itself is mathematically correct — which matches the plan's own design constraint ("the LLM never decides which number is larger"), and this finding is direct primary-source support for keeping that constraint hard, not aspirational.

**Proposed constrained prompt outline:**
```
SYSTEM: You are classifying ONE student's typed explanation for why they placed decimal
number A at a specific point on a 0-1 number line, when the deterministic checker has
already determined the placement is WRONG (ground truth: A vs B ordering is known,
supplied to you, never inferred by you).

INPUT:
- decimal_a, decimal_b (ground truth ordering supplied)
- learner_placement (which one the learner marked larger — supplied, not inferred by you)
- learner_text (free text, may be empty)

TASK: Output exactly one label from this closed set:
  L      - reasoning consistent with "more digits / longer = larger"
  S      - reasoning consistent with "more decimal places = smaller" / small-unit thinking
  MONEY  - reasoning framed via currency/money equivalence
  SLIP   - reasoning states CORRECT magnitude understanding but describes an execution/
           reading/click/orientation error separate from the stated belief
  GUESS  - no discernible numeric reasoning; explicit uncertainty
  UNCLEAR- off-topic, empty, or does not fit any of the above with reasonable confidence

Also output a confidence score 0.0-1.0. If confidence < 0.6, output UNCLEAR regardless
of your best guess.

CONSTRAINTS:
- Never state or imply which decimal is actually larger — that is already known and is
  not your job.
- Do not generate encouragement, scoring, or next-step text in this call — a separate
  call handles learner-facing feedback wording, gated on this label.
- Keep output to the label + confidence only (structured, e.g. JSON) for reliable parsing.
```

**Fallback path:** if the LLM call fails, times out, returns a malformed/unparseable response, or returns confidence < 0.6 → fall back to the deterministic keyword+pattern table (rows 1/2/5/6/9-style rules) to assign the closest of L/S/MONEY/GUESS/UNCLEAR; if the deterministic table also can't confidently match, default to UNCLEAR and route the learner to a generic "explain your thinking again" prompt rather than blocking progress. This preserves "deterministic math is source of truth" (the placement's correctness is never LLM-decided) while the LLM's genuine, narrow, fallback-safe job is the harder free-text classification task above it.

## 5. Demo spine — 3:00 timeline

**0:00–0:20 | Hook + misconception surfaces.** Synthetic learner profile "Maya, Grade 4" is placing decimal cards on a 0-1 line. She places 0.45 to the right of 0.8 (confidently, quickly). On-screen the deterministic checker instantly shows the correct positions (0.8 sits right of 0.45) — no narration needed, the visual contradiction between her placement and the revealed correct one *is* the hook.

**0:20–0:45 | Zoom reveals *why* it's wrong (representation, not lecture).** Learner (or demo operator) zooms the 0-1 line into tenths, then hundredths. 0.8 visibly expands to "0.80" occupying the same physical point; 0.45 is visibly, unambiguously to its left once the hundredths grid renders. This is the "essential game action embodies the math" beat — zooming *is* place value, not a quiz question about place value.

**0:45–1:15 | Targeted AI support: the erroneous example.** The game now shows "a classmate's" (synthetic, AI-generated) wrong sort that mirrors Maya's own inferred rule (L) — e.g., "0.62 vs 0.7, classmate said 0.62 is bigger." Learner is asked to find and fix the classmate's error and explain why. On-screen: a small "AI-generated for you" badge or similar, so the judge sees the AI's job is scoped to generating this personalized wrong example, not judging Maya's math.

**1:15–1:45 | Self-correction: learner explains, AI classifies (not judges correctness).** Learner fixes the classmate's placement and types a one-line explanation ("hundredths are smaller pieces, 62 hundredths of one is still less than 7 tenths" or similar). On screen, briefly show the constrained classifier output (e.g., a small internal label like "reasoning: positional/valid" vs a slip-only view) gating whether the game moves Maya to a transfer item or serves another erroneous example — this is the moment that makes the AI's "genuine responsibility" legible to a judge without narration explaining the whole architecture.

**1:45–2:25 | Transfer.** Three quick beats, each defeating simple length-based rules: (a) 0.7 vs 0.65 (same digit count trap doesn't exist here — both look like the counter-rule so this is a stretch/hard item), (b) money framing — 0.5 vs 0.45, and (c) measurement framing — 1.05m vs 1.5m. Show Maya correctly placing/ordering these without hesitation, demonstrating the rule (not just the specific pair) transferred.

**2:25–2:50 | The one clear AI contribution, named directly (brief on-screen text or single VO line).** A single caption or brief narration: "The AI never decides which number is bigger — the math engine does that. The AI's job: generate a personalized wrong example from Maya's own mistake, and read her explanation to know when she's really got it." ## 6. Top 5 reasons a judge might reject this, and mitigations

1. **"The number line + comparison is a well-worn EdTech pattern; where's the novelty?"** — Many existing apps (Khan Academy, IXL, DreamBox) already do decimal number-line placement. Mitigation: the pitch must foreground the *erroneous-example-generation-from-the-learner's-own-inferred-rule* + *free-text-explanation-gating* loop as the differentiator, not the number line itself — the number line is the (necessary, not sufficient) representation; the AI-personalized erroneous example is the actual product idea.

2. **"Grade 4-5 is arguably too old/narrow for a 'K-5' hackathon and the supporting intervention research (McLaren 2015/2017) is middle-school (6-8), not upper-elementary."** Verified true: both McLaren studies you're leaning on for effect sizes are middle school, not Grade 4-5. Mitigation: be upfront in the pitch that the erroneous-examples evidence base is from slightly older students and frame the Grade 4-5 application as an evidence-informed extension, not a direct replication — plus lean on the Steinle & Stacey data (which *does* cover Grade 4-5 directly) for the misconception-prevalence claim, and the Common Core/NCERT-old-curriculum citations for age-appropriateness of the content itself.

3. **"The core demo-able win (delayed-test advantage of erroneous examples) is invisible in a live 3-minute demo — so why should judges believe the loop 'works' at all?"** This is the plan's own stated weakness, and research confirms it (McLaren 2015's effect is specifically a *delayed*, not immediate, effect). Mitigation: don't claim the demo *proves* the delayed-learning effect; instead demo the *mechanism* (the loop runs, the AI generates a relevant erroneous example, the classifier gates progress correctly on good vs. bad explanations) and cite the published effect size as the reason the mechanism should be expected to produce that outcome over time — separate "this loop is well-designed and grounded in research" from "this 3-minute clip proves learning happened."

4. **"An LLM judging a child's math reasoning is exactly the kind of AI overreach judges are trained to be suspicious of, especially given documented ~75% LLM accuracy on this exact decimal/number-line domain."** Mitigation: this is actually a strength if framed correctly — cite the arXiv paper's 75% figure yourself, proactively, as the reason the design keeps a human-authored deterministic fallback and never lets the LLM adjudicate correctness (only classify the *style* of an already-known-wrong explanation). Owning the limitation preempts the objection.

5. **"The rule classification (L/S/A/U) that the whole personalization loop depends on is not session-stable — Steinle & Stacey's own data shows ~50%+ of students don't retest into the same code."** This is a real, primary-source-backed risk: if "Maya's inferred rule" flips between one session and the next, the AI-generated erroneous example could target the wrong misconception. Mitigation: scope the claim to *within-session* consistency only (infer the rule from the current session's placements, regenerate the inference each session rather than persisting a stored "this student is an L-type" label across days) — this sidesteps the long-timescale instability finding, which was measured across ~8-month gaps, not within a single sitting (an honest gap: short-timescale stability is genuinely untested, so treat within-session inference as the safe scope, not a proven-stable long-term profile).

## 7. Verdict

**Build-readiness: 3.5 / 5.**

Reasoning:
- The misconception itself (L/S rule-based decimal comparison) is the **best-evidenced, most rigorously classified misconception in all of elementary/middle math education** — the Steinle & Stacey Decimal Comparison Test is a genuinely deterministic, primary-source-verified diagnostic instrument, which is an unusually strong foundation for a hackathon entry ("one observable misconception" requirement is trivially satisfied and well-documented).
- The zoomable-number-line representation is defensible and matches the mathematics (place value made spatially manipulable), but its *direct* evidence base at the target age is a single N=6 qualitative study — thin, though not contradicted.
- The erroneous-example + free-text-explanation-classification mechanic is well-precedented (McLaren line of work) but that precedent is middle-school, not Grade 4-5, and its flagship effect (delayed-test gains) is undemoable live — a structural, not fixable-by-more-build-time, limitation of the format.
- The AI-value case is the strongest part of the design *because it is narrow and falsifiable*: the arXiv 2306.16639 finding (75% correctness ceiling, specifically weak on number-line/place-value items) is direct, on-topic, verified evidence that (a) the LLM should not judge correctness (matches the plan) and (b) the LLM's real, defensible job (classifying free-text explanations, generating personalized erroneous examples) is scoped correctly.
- 6-day build risk is moderate-to-high: a working zoomable number line + deterministic L/S/A/U-style classifier + LLM-backed erroneous-example generator + constrained explanation classifier + fallback authoring, all polished enough for a clean 3-minute demo, is a lot of surface area for one person, though each piece individually is buildable (the DCT's 10 "core items" table is a ready-made deterministic backbone to lift directly).

**Three most important open questions:**
1. **Is within-session rule inference (minutes, not months) stable enough to drive "generate the erroneous example matching the learner's own rule" — or will the personalization frequently mis-target because a student's first few placements don't reliably predict their next few?** No evidence found either way at this timescale; this is closer to a build-time risk to test empirically on day 1-2 than a literature question.
2. **Can the erroneous-example bank + classifier be authored well enough in the available time to survive the Mínguez-Pardo (2024) finding that erroneous-example benefit is proficiency-dependent** — i.e., does the game need a *branch* for "guess"/"unclear"-labeled learners (who may need a worked correct example instead of another erroneous one) to avoid the disconfirmed "erroneous examples help everyone equally" assumption?
3. **Which curriculum/notation profile does the demo actually target for the judges** — US Common Core Grade 4 framing (decimal point, hundredths) is the cleanest, most literature-supported choice; an India-facing framing would need either to shift to "Class 5-6" language or accept the game reads as slightly advanced-for-grade under the current NCERT sequence. This should be settled before scripting the demo narration, since the brief requires notation/curriculum acknowledgment, not silence.
