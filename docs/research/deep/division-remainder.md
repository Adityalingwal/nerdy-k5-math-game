## TASK 1 — Plan-critical evidence, verified source by source

### 1a. Silver, Shapiro & Deutsch (1993/1990) — the "bus problem"

- **Citation (confirmed via ERIC preprint of the JRME article):** Silver, E.A., Shapiro, L.J., & Deutsch, A. (1993). "Sense making and the solution of division problems involving remainders: An examination of middle school students' solution processes and their interpretations of solutions." *Journal for Research in Mathematics Education*, 24(2), 117–135. A 1990 LRDC (University of Pittsburgh) technical report version is on ERIC as ED342647 (SE 052 501) — this is the version I actually read in full text.
- **Verification label: FULL-TEXT VERIFIED** (read the ERIC PDF abstract page directly, not just a citation snippet).
- **Exact sample:** N = 195 (not ~200 — that figure floating around secondary sources is an approximation), sixth, seventh, and eighth graders ("middle school," not "middle school broadly" — ages roughly 11–14) at **one large urban US middle school**, ~40% Caucasian / 60% African-American, all ability levels, taught by volunteer teachers.
- **Task:** each student got a practical division-with-remainder problem (the "army bus" problem: buses hold 36 soldiers, transport 1128 soldiers — how many buses?), in three versions varying whether the true remainder was equal to, greater than, or less than half the divisor.
- **Exact quantitative results (from the abstract, verbatim):**
  - Over 70% of students applied division or another appropriate procedure.
  - **61%** obtained a *correct numerical* answer (quotient + remainder correct).
  - **45%** of subjects gave the augmented-quotient (round-up) answer — i.e., the contextually sensible answer for this problem.
  - "Few students brought 'real world' knowledge to bear on the problem and on making appropriate interpretations of their numerical process."
- **Read on the claim used in the brief ("what fraction gave sensible answers"):** the sensible-answer rate is **45%**, not a lower/higher number sometimes quoted informally. This is meaningfully more than half of the successful dividers, and meaningfully less than "most kids get it right." It supports the misconception (a majority-minus fail to interpret) but is less dramatic than the "3.4 buses" framing implies if quoted as "almost nobody gets it."
- **Caveat for the pitch:** this is middle schoolers (11-14), not the target 8-10 age band. It establishes the *phenomenon exists and persists into middle school*, not that it exists at Grade 3-4 with this severity.

### 1b. Pacheco-Muñoz, Nava-Lobato, Juárez-López & Ponce de León-Palacios (2022)

- **Citation:** "Division problems with remainder: A study on strategies and interpretations with fourth grade Mexican students." *Mathematics Teaching Research Journal*, 14(5), Winter 2022, pp.159–176 (ERIC EJ1382676).
- **Verification label: FULL-TEXT VERIFIED** (read the full PDF, pages 1–10 including method, table of problems, and transcript excerpts).
- **Exact sample:** N = 50 fourth-grade students (ages 9–10) at **one private school in Puebla, Mexico**, selected by convenience; 7 of the 50 were also interviewed individually (audio-recorded, transcribed).
- **Design:** Qualitative, descriptive/interpretive — a 3-item written questionnaire (2 quotitive/measurement-division items, 1 partitive item) plus a clinical interview with 7 students. **Not an RCT, not a quasi-experiment, no inferential statistics, no aggregate "% realistic" table was found in the pages reviewed** — findings are reported as qualitative response categories (realistic-with-division, realistic-without-division, unrealistic-applying-division, other) illustrated with worked examples and quotes, not as percentages across the full N=50.
- **Notably close to the candidate's own scenario:** one of their three problems is literally "22 soccer players want to travel by cab, each cab carries 4 players, how many cabs do they need?" — i.e., people + capacity + remainder + round-up, functionally identical to the candidate's "17 people / 5-seat vehicle" mechanic. This is strong face-validity evidence that the exact scenario type has been used in real research with this exact age group (9-10, Grade 4).
- **Qualitative finding relevant to the pitch:** they document all four theorized response types actually occurring in this age group, including a student (S4) who correctly divided 122 candles by 30/box, got quotient 4 remainder 2, and then — under interview pressure — still initially said "5 boxes... no wait, 4" and reasoned that the 2 leftover candles "can't go because the boxes are already full," landing on "buy another box" only when prompted. This is a near-verbatim empirical instance of the target misconception in a Grade 4, non-US population.
- **Caveat:** convenience sample of 50 from a single private school — not generalizable, and the paper itself does not quantify prevalence (no "% of Grade 4 kids get this wrong" number exists in this paper to cite).

### 1c. Spanish secondary students DWR study (Springer, doi 10.1007/BF03178766)

- **Verification label: ABSTRACT-LEVEL** (Springer article is paywalled/login-gated; WebFetch was redirected to an IdP login wall and could not retrieve the full text. Information below is from search-engine-indexed abstract text, not a primary read.)
- **Reported sample:** 45 Spanish secondary students, ages 12–13.
- **Design:** Two division situations (Equal Groups, Comparison) × four remainder types (Remainder-Not-Divisible / round-up, Remainder-Divisible / round-down, Remainder-as-Result, Readjusted-Quotient-by-Partial-Increments), aimed at separating "bad initial representation" from "bad final interpretation" as sources of error, and testing whether remainder types cluster into two blocks.
- **Relevance to pitch:** supports the "two-goal-label version might already capture most of the variance" collapse-risk noted in the brief — the paper's own research question (can these be reduced to two blocks?) is exactly the reduction the brief worries would kill the AI's added value. **This should be read as mild evidence *against* needing more than 2 categories**, not as support for a rich N-way taxonomy.
- **Confidence:** medium — abstract-level only, could not verify actual reported percentages or which direction the "two blocks" question was answered.

### 1d. "Can younger students succeed where older students fail?" (third graders)

- **Citation:** Li, Y., & Silver, E.A. (2000). "Can younger students succeed where older students fail? An examination of third graders' solutions of a division-with-remainder (DWR) problem." *Journal of Mathematical Behavior*, 19(2), 233–246.
- **Verification label: ABSTRACT-LEVEL** (ResearchGate page returned HTTP 403; details below are from search-engine snippet/secondary summary, not a primary read of the article itself. Treat the specific numbers with caution.)
- **Reported design:** 14 third graders who had **not yet been taught the formal division algorithm** solved a DWR problem using informal, context-tied strategies (direct modeling, counting, repeated addition) rather than the standard algorithm.
- **Reported finding:** these third graders performed comparatively well — better, on a sense-making basis, than the middle schoolers in Silver, Shapiro & Deutsch (1993) who *had* been taught the algorithm — because the third graders' informal strategies stayed tied to the problem context, whereas middle-schoolers' fluent-but-decontextualized algorithm use let them "solve" the arithmetic while losing the situational meaning.
- **This is the single most important — and double-edged — piece of evidence for the pitch.** It suggests: (a) Grade 3 kids *can* reason sensibly about remainders before they're taught to divide formally, which is good news for building a Grade 3-4 game around informal reasoning; but (b) it also implies the misconception may be *manufactured by premature algorithm teaching*, i.e., not a stable "developmental" misconception to remediate but an artifact of how division is currently taught — which changes the framing from "kids have a bug" to "instruction creates a bug, and this game either avoids the bug (if used before/alongside algorithm teaching) or is remediating self-inflicted institutional damage." Either framing is usable for a hackathon pitch, but they are different claims and the demo narration should pick one and be consistent.
- **Sample size (14) is very small** — this is a case-study-scale qualitative study, not a claim about "third graders in general."

### 1e. RCT / quasi-experiment on teaching remainder interpretation, Grades 3-5

- **Finding: NO RCT OR QUASI-EXPERIMENT WAS FOUND** specifically testing an intervention that teaches "interpreting remainders" to Grades 3-5 with a control condition and outcome measure. Searches for "division with remainder intervention elementary," "interpreting remainders instruction study," and general RCT/quasi-experiment terms surfaced only: lesson-plan resources (Scribd, Kentucky DOE, Illustrative Mathematics blog posts) that are curricular guidance, not research studies; the qualitative Pacheco-Muñoz study (1c above); and general math word-problem-solving intervention meta-analyses that are not specific to remainder interpretation (see 2b below).
- **Verification label: SEARCH EXHAUSTED, NEGATIVE RESULT** — I could not find a controlled study of teaching remainder-interpretation specifically. This is a real evidence gap, not just a search-effort limitation, given how narrowly the search terms targeted this exact question across multiple phrasings.
- **Implication for the pitch:** the claim "an AI-mediated game will *improve* remainder-interpretation skill" has **zero direct causal evidence behind it** — every relevant study cited here is descriptive/diagnostic (what errors look like), not evaluative (whether any intervention, let alone this one, fixes them). This should be stated plainly in the pitch as a hypothesis, not dressed up as "research shows this works."

### 1f. Verschaffel/Greer/De Corte "suspension of sense-making," cross-national

- **Citation:** Yoshida, H., Verschaffel, L., & De Corte, E. (1997). "Realistic considerations in solving problematic word problems: Do Japanese and Belgian children have the same difficulties?" *Learning and Instruction*, 7(4), 329–338.
- **Verification label: ABSTRACT-LEVEL** (found via ScienceDirect listing + secondary summaries; full text not fetched, paywalled).
- **Reported design/sample:** ~91 Japanese fifth graders (and a matched Belgian sample from the broader Verschaffel/De Corte/Lasure 1994 program) given a mixed set of "standard" (cleanly modelable) and "problematic" (realistically ill-posed, e.g., division-with-remainder-style) word problems.
- **Reported finding:** Japanese pupils showed the **same** strong tendency as Belgian pupils to suspend real-world sense-making and give the "clean" arithmetic answer to problematic items — i.e., this is not a Belgian-schooling artifact; it replicates cross-nationally (Belgium and Japan, two very differently-taught systems). This is the strongest available evidence that the underlying phenomenon (suspension of sense-making on division-with-remainder-type items) is **not culturally or curriculum-idiosyncratic**, which is directly useful for the pitch's "geography-neutral core mechanic" claim — the *misconception* itself travels across cultures even if surface content (buses vs. rickshaws) must be localized.
- **Broader source family:** Verschaffel, De Corte & Lasure (1994) and the edited volume Verschaffel, Greer & De Corte (2000) *Making Sense of Word Problems* are the root of this program; multiple replications in different countries are referenced in secondary literature but I did not independently verify each one (would exceed the search budget).

## TASK 2 — Disconfirming evidence

### 2a. Word problems become reading/language tests

- **Source:** "Word Problem Solving in Contemporary Math Education: A Plea for Reading Comprehension Skills Training," PMC4756284 (published academic article, PubMed Central).
- **Verification label: FULL-TEXT VERIFIED** (fetched and read the article body).
- **Key finding:** Reading comprehension correlated **r = 0.59, p < .001** with performance on Realistic Mathematics Education (RME)-style word-problem tests. Critically, students classified as "successful" math-modelers (i.e., who had adequate mental representation skills for the math itself) still failed specifically on *semantically/linguistically complex* ("marked") problem phrasings — for this subgroup, reading comprehension correlated **r = −0.40, p = .01** with performance on marked-inconsistent items, and a large interaction effect (partial η² = 0.30) showed language complexity, not math ability, was driving their errors.
- **Direct relevance:** this is a strong, on-point, full-text-verified disconfirming signal for the plan risk explicitly flagged in the brief. A game whose "revealing action" is followed by asking an 8-10-year-old to *type a written justification* ("we need 4 buses because...") risks measuring writing/reading fluency, not division sense-making — exactly the confound this paper documents. **This is the single most load-bearing piece of disconfirming evidence in this research pass** and should directly shape the AI-judge design (see Task 4): the judge should be evaluated for whether it's robust to short, ungrammatical, or non-native-English phrasing, not just semantically clear prose.

### 2b. "Realistic" interventions with small/no effects

- Meta-analytic evidence found (Myers, Witzel, Powell, Li, Pigott, Xin & Hughes, 2022, *Review of Educational Research*-family meta-analysis, and a 2025 ScienceDirect systematic review/meta-analysis) shows word-problem-solving interventions for elementary students *overall* have a reported large average effect — **but** the same body of evidence flags that **studies with weaker methodological quality (researcher-made outcome measures, weak sampling controls) showed inflated effects relative to studies using standardized measures and tighter designs.** This is a general caution about the whole word-problem-intervention literature's effect-size credibility, not remainder-specific, but it directly undercuts any temptation to cite "word problem interventions work great" as support without checking whether the underlying evidence used a strong design.
- **Verification label:** ABSTRACT-LEVEL (search-engine summaries of the meta-analyses' abstracts, not full-text read — would need dedicated fetches that were out of budget).
- No study was found evaluating a "realistic problem" intervention that reported a *null or negative* effect outright; the disconfirming signal here is about *measurement/quality confounds inflating effects*, not about "realistic problems don't help at all."

### 2c. Remainder interpretation as a language/pragmatics issue, not purely mathematical

- Direct, dedicated critique literature specifically framing "interpreting remainders" as a pragmatics-of-language problem (in the Grice/conversational-implicature sense) was **not found** in this search pass — searches returned only pedagogical/how-to content (task cards, worksheets, blog posts about the four remainder-interpretation strategies), not academic critique.
- The closest substantive relative found is the Verschaffel/Greer/De Corte program's broader argument (referenced throughout 1f and Pacheco-Muñoz's citations) that word-problem-solving is governed by an implicit classroom "Word Problem Game" whose norms are social/pragmatic (what counts as an acceptable answer in *this* classroom culture) rather than purely computational — this is adjacent to a pragmatics argument but is about classroom register/norms, not linguistic pragmatics of remainder problems specifically.
- **Verification label:** NEGATIVE RESULT for a dedicated "it's pragmatics, not math" paper on remainders specifically; the adjacent "Word Problem Game" argument is well-established (abstract-level) and should be treated as the honest proxy for this claim in the pitch, not overstated as a direct citation.

**Is Grade 3-4 defensible as "K-5 foundational arithmetic"?**
Across all three curricula, **explicit interpretation of remainders is consistently placed at Grade/Year 4, not Grade/Year 3** — US Common Core names it exactly at 4.OA.3; England splits it across Year 5-6 (ages 9-11), later than the US; India's Class 4 (age 9-10) roughly matches the US. **None of the three curricula treat "interpreting remainders" as Grade 3 content.** This means the candidate's own framing ("Grades 3-4") is defensible only at its upper edge (Grade 4/age 9-10) and is curricularly premature at its lower edge (Grade 3/age 8) — Grade 3 children are still expected to be building division fluency without remainders in the US scheme, so a game targeting Grade 3 for this specific skill would be **ahead of standard scope-and-sequence in every curriculum checked.** This isn't disqualifying (the Li & Silver 2000 study, 1d above, suggests younger kids can reason about this informally, arguably even better than older kids taught the algorithm) — but it does mean "Grades 3-4" should probably be narrowed to "Grade 4 (age 9-10), usable as an early/enrichment experience for strong Grade 3 students" rather than pitched as squarely on-grade for both grades simultaneously.

### 4b. Honest verdict — does the LLM add value beyond rules, and exactly where?

**Yes, but only in a narrow, specific band of cases — not across the board.** Concretely:

- Rows 4, 6, 7: a deterministic system (numeric-answer check + simple presence/absence of scaffolding keywords) is **as good or better** than an LLM judge. Don't spend AI budget here.
- Rows 1, 2: trivial keyword matching genuinely suffices; an LLM is unnecessary overhead.
- Rows 3, 8, 9, 10: this is where the LLM earns its place — **specifically when the justification (a) references facts that contradict the stated constraints in a way only semantic understanding catches (row 3), (b) contains a coherent but non-canonical decomposition strategy that deserves differentiated praise, not just pass/fail (row 8), (c) contains self-correction or scope-extension that a rigid parser will mis-anchor on (row 9), or (d) is in a different language/dialect than the rule set was authored for (row 10).**
- **The honest, narrow claim for the pitch is:** "A keyword/rule system can correctly grade the *majority* of simple justifications (the deterministic quotient/remainder computation is never in question — that's always rule-owned) but reliably fails on justifications that plausibly satisfy the goal via reasoning the rule author didn't anticipate, or that superficially match keywords while violating the situation's constraints. The LLM's genuine, defensible job is exactly this narrow adjudication task — not generating the "right answer" (deterministic), not scoring correctness of arithmetic (deterministic), but judging whether *free-form natural-language reasoning* about a *known, deterministic* situation is internally consistent with that situation's stated constraints." This is a real and legitimate AI responsibility — but it is a **classification/adjudication task over a small, closed label set (4 labels)**, not an open-ended tutoring or generation task, and the pitch should be careful not to oversell it as more than that.
- **Fairness risk flagged in the brief is real and confirmed by 2a and row 10 above:** grading 9-year-olds' free text risks conflating writing ability / English fluency with math understanding. Mitigation: (i) allow multiple modalities for the justification beyond free text (e.g., picking a pre-built reason chip AND optionally typing more), (ii) design the judge prompt to explicitly discount grammar/spelling and focus only on whether the stated quantity/action is consistent with the situation, (iii) test the judge on deliberately broken-English and code-mixed strings (as in row 10) before the demo, and (iv) always have the safe fallback (chip-based reasons) be the primary path, with free text as an enrichment, not a gate.

### 4c. Situation-generation role — can an LLM reliably generate situations with a KNOWN interpretation type?

- This is a **harder and riskier AI claim than the judging claim**, and the research above gives no direct evidence either way (no paper tests LLM situation-generation — this is a product-design question, not a literature question).
- **Design risk:** generating a *fresh* situation ("17 kids, 5-seat rickshaws" → "23 mangoes, boxes of 6") that is guaranteed to be a *clean* instance of a specific interpretation type (round-up vs round-down vs fractional vs remainder-is-the-answer) requires the LLM to correctly compute divisibility and remainder size *before* it ever reaches the deterministic engine — i.e., the LLM's own arithmetic must be checked, not trusted. LLMs are known to be unreliable at exactly this kind of small-but-exact arithmetic consistency-checking on the first pass.
- **Recommended constrained-generation + verification pattern:**
  1. **Deterministic system picks the numbers first** (dividend, divisor, and therefore quotient/remainder/interpretation-type are fixed *before* any LLM call — e.g., roll dividend=17, divisor=5, tag "round-up" because remainder>0 and situation-class="discrete indivisible units").
  2. **LLM is given the numbers and the required interpretation-type label as fixed inputs**, and asked only to *dress* them in a fresh, localized surface story ("write a one-sentence situation where 17 of [locale-configured noun] must be grouped into containers of 5, and the leftover must trigger [round-up / round-down / fractional] framing"), not to invent the numbers or determine the interpretation type itself.
  3. **Deterministic system re-parses the LLM's output** (or, more robustly, requires the LLM to fill a structured template — noun, container-noun, capacity — rather than free prose) and cross-checks that the plugged-in numbers still match the pre-committed dividend/divisor before the situation is shown to the learner.
  4. **Fallback:** if the LLM's generated surface story fails the re-parse/cross-check (e.g., it hallucinated different numbers or an incompatible noun), silently discard it and serve a situation from the authored bank instead — the learner never sees a broken situation.
- **Constrained prompt outline:**
  ```
  You will localize a math situation template. Do not change any numbers.
  Fixed inputs: quantity=17, group_size=5, interpretation_type=round_up,
  locale=en-IN, theme=transport.
  Task: return JSON {noun: <plural noun for the 17 items, must be transportable
  people/objects>, container_noun: <plural noun for the group_size unit>,
  vehicle_or_container_singular: <singular>, sentence: <one natural sentence
  in en-IN English stating that "quantity" "noun" need to be grouped into
  "container_noun" of "group_size" each, using ONLY the given numbers>}.
  Do not restate or alter 17 or 5. Do not resolve the problem. Do not mention
  the interpretation type.
  ```
  The deterministic system then checks the returned JSON's implied numbers via string/number extraction against the fixed inputs, and only renders the situation if they match exactly.
- **Bottom line:** situation-generation is plausible as an AI contribution **only if the numbers and interpretation-type are locked deterministically first and the LLM is constrained to a verified-template fill, never given freedom over the numbers.** If the build runs short on time, this piece (situation generation) is the one to cut first — the authored situation bank (per the brief's own stated fallback) is a fully adequate substitute and arguably safer for a 3-minute demo, whereas the judging role (4b) is the more defensible and demo-worthy "genuine AI responsibility" to keep.

## TASK 6 — Top 5 reject reasons and mitigations

1. **"This is really a reading/writing test, not a math test" (strongest risk — backed by full-text-verified r=0.59 correlation, Task 2a).** Mitigation: show in the demo (or explicitly state) that the primary interaction path is non-verbal (drag people into vehicles, press go, see the block) and that typed justification is an *optional enrichment layer* with a chip-based fallback; show the judge explicitly discounting grammar/spelling.
2. **"There's no evidence any intervention actually improves this skill" (backed by Task 1e negative-result search).** Mitigation: be upfront in the pitch that this is a *diagnostic and practice* loop, not a proven-efficacy intervention — hackathon framing should claim "surfaces and repeatedly confronts the misconception with immediate, situation-grounded feedback," not "proven to raise scores."
3. **"The AI claim collapses to 2 fixed labels / could be an if-else" (explicitly named as a risk in the brief, and reinforced by the Spanish secondary-school paper's own research question about collapsing remainder types into two blocks, Task 1c).** Mitigation: the demo must show a case (like row 3 or row 9 in Task 4a) where a keyword/rule system visibly gets it wrong and the constrained LLM gets it right — don't just assert AI value, show the failure mode of the naive alternative on-screen.
4. **"Grade 3-4 framing is curricularly premature" (backed by Task 3 — no curriculum places remainder-interpretation at Grade 3).** Mitigation: reframe target band as "Grade 4, with informal/pre-algorithm entry points suitable for advanced Grade 3" — cite Li & Silver (2000) explicitly as support for *why* pre-algorithm Grade 3 reasoning is worth including, not despite the curriculum gap but because of the finding that informal reasoning may outperform premature algorithm fluency.
5. **"The evidence base is thin/old/small-N and largely non-US" (backed across Tasks 1a–1d — largest N is 195 middle-schoolers from 1990/1993; the Grade 3-4-specific studies are N=50 and N=14).** Mitigation: don't oversell "extensively researched" — pitch it as "a well-documented, decades-replicated *phenomenon* (cross-national per Yoshida et al. 1997) with a *specific, novel* game mechanic and AI role that nobody has evaluated yet" — i.e., own the fact that the *mechanic* is the hackathon's original contribution, not a validated pedagogy being merely digitized.

## TASK 7 — Verdict

**Build-readiness: 3/5.**

Reasoning: The misconception is real, well-documented across decades and countries (Silver et al. 1993 full-text-verified at N=195; Yoshida et al. 1997 cross-national replication; Pacheco-Muñoz 2022 gives an almost-literal match to the candidate's own "people into vehicles" scenario at the exact target age). The core mechanic (pack-and-dispatch, remainder physically visible, blocked "go") plausibly does embody the math rather than quizzing about it, satisfying the hard constraint. But three things hold this back from a 4-5: (a) there is no evidence anywhere that an intervention of this kind actually improves the targeted skill — the whole causal chain from "game" to "learning" is unstudied; (b) the strongest disconfirming evidence found (r=0.59 reading-comprehension confound) lands directly on the part of the design (typed justification + LLM judging) that is supposed to be the "genuine AI contribution," meaning the riskiest design element and the most evidence-vulnerable element are the same element; (c) Grade 3 is curricularly premature by every standard checked, which is a fixable framing problem but currently a mismatch between the stated age band and the actual evidence/standards.

**Three most important open questions:**

1. **Can the judge be made robust to short, ungrammatical, or code-mixed 8-10-year-old text without silently reverting to "unclear" so often that it's useless** — this needs to be prototyped and stress-tested (with inputs like rows 3, 9, 10 from Task 4a) before deciding the free-text-judging path is worth the build time, versus leaning more heavily on the chip-based fallback as the primary loop.
2. **Should the target band be narrowed to Grade 4 only** (dropping Grade 3 from the headline framing, per Task 3), and if so, does that change the "K-5 foundational arithmetic" framing enough to matter to judges who might independently know the standards?
3. **Is situation-generation (Task 4c) worth building at all in a 6-day window**, given that (a) it's the shakier of the two candidate AI roles, (b) the authored situation bank is an admitted adequate fallback, and (c) the demo's clearest AI-value moment (Task 5, 1:15–1:55) depends on judging, not generation — i.e., is there a version of this pitch that drops situation-generation entirely and puts 100% of the AI budget and demo time into making the judge visibly, defensibly better than a naive rule system?
