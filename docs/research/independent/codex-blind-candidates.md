Evidence is kept in four categories:

- **C — Curriculum expectation:** what a named curriculum asks learners to know. It is neither prevalence evidence nor proof that a proposed game works.
- **O — Observed behavior:** empirical learner findings in a stated population. An association or qualitative interview is not an intervention result.
- **I — Intervention evidence:** outcomes from instruction in the source's conditions. Transfer to this game, this duration, or another language remains unproven.
- **P/H — Product inference or hypothesis:** every proposed mechanic, hint, diagnostic interpretation, AI responsibility, demo, and mastery rule below. None has been tested with learners.

“Body-verified” means relevant methods/results passages were inspected, not that the entire paper was audited. “Abstract-level” includes figures available only in an abstract or search summary. Any research number with that weaker verification is explicitly marked **(abstract-level)**. Numbers in invented problems, demo timings, and the selection rubric are design examples, not research statistics.

## What the evidence can support across candidates

The U.S. IES/WWC elementary intervention guide supports systematic instruction, clear mathematical language, carefully selected representations, and number lines. Its setting is supplemental intervention; it does not establish that a short autonomous AI game duplicates teacher-led instruction. [S10](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/26)

Manipulatives are not sufficient by themselves. A meta-analysis covering **55 studies and 7,237 participants (abstract-level)** found benefits over abstract-symbol-only instruction, but transfer and justification effects were smaller than retention effects. It spans kindergarten through college and concerns concrete materials, so it cannot directly validate digital dragging for one elementary misconception. [S11](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-the-efficacy-of-teaching-mathematics-with-conc/)

Number-line performance has a substantial research base, including a correlational synthesis of **162 studies and 33,101 participants** whose methods/results were inspected. Association with mathematics outcomes is not evidence that training any number-line game causes broad improvement. Its international sample is still dominated by particular countries and English-language publications. [S12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11753455/)

A visually corrected state is an action result. An unhinted novel task is a provisional transfer signal. Durable learning requires later assessment. A three-minute adult enactment establishes none of the latter by itself.

## Shared design boundaries applying to every candidate

These are comparison assumptions, not accepted product decisions.

**Learner and access.** Entry is based on prerequisites and repeated observable behavior, not age alone. Curriculum grades are orientation labels. An unfamiliar control, vocabulary, or representation must be checked before attributing an error to mathematics. Every drag should have an equivalent select-and-place action; color should not be the sole carrier of meaning. There is no speed-based mastery requirement.

**One loop.** A learner constructs or changes a mathematical object, commits a prediction, observes consequences, explains or demonstrates a reason, receives contingent support, and tries an unseen variation. Do not combine these fifteen ideas into a curriculum.

**Math authority.** The deterministic system owns quantities, units, legal transformations, exact integer/rational arithmetic, visual scale, correct outcomes, and progression conditions. It permits mathematically revealing wrong proposals, then displays their actual consequences; it must not silently prevent every error and mistake constrained success for understanding.

**AI boundary.** A plausible responsibility is interpreting an unexpected mathematical explanation or self-authored strategy together with action history, then choosing a discriminating probe or responding to the learner's specific claim. That is a hypothesis of useful semantic adaptation, not proof of AI necessity. Known error codes, arithmetic checking, difficulty changes, and a finite hint ladder belong to ordinary rules. Translation, encouragement, question decoration, and restating a correct answer receive no AI credit.

The proposed AI output is a bounded suggestion linked to observed evidence and a validated task/hint family. It cannot determine mathematical truth, invent mastery, infer a clinical condition, or insist it knows why a child erred. A conflicting or low-confidence interpretation yields “show me” plus a deterministic diagnostic task. Free-form mathematical feedback is harder to verify than equations; an inability to constrain it safely is a reason to reject the AI design, not permission to trust it.

**Data and demo.** Use only adult-authored synthetic learner traces and invented mathematical explanations for development and demonstrations. No real student records, identifiable media, child names, voices, or personal histories are imported from papers or collected. Prospective language interaction does not imply permission to process real child text: no child trial is assumed; any future trial must satisfy the project's consent and data constraints. No accounts or persistent profiles are needed. A demonstration must identify synthetic enactment honestly and show an actual functioning product if one is later built.

**Geography.** Abstract counters, lengths, equal groups, and quantities avoid currencies, local sports, and culturally specific story knowledge. This does not make instruction universal. English number names, “take away” versus “difference,” “times as many,” “fourths” versus “quarters,” decimal separators, division notation, reading direction, and curriculum sequencing require local review. A number line's increasing direction must be explicitly taught rather than inferred from interface reading direction. Equal allocation is an explicit mathematical rule, not an assumption that all cultures define fairness identically. England's programme and U.S. Common Core provide two reference sequences; neither stands for every country. [S09](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study), [S30](https://www.nuffieldfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Review-of-research-Griffiths-Teaching-fractions-and-decimals-to-children-aged-3-to-11.pdf)

## Full candidate set — no ranking

### C01 — Exact-Set Dispatch

**Target learner and barrier.** A school-entry learner can recite a short count sequence and move one counter at a time, but cannot consistently construct a requested quantity. The narrow barrier is failure to connect the final count word to the size of the completed set; fluent recitation is not sufficient evidence of cardinal understanding.

**Why consequential.** Exact-set construction is needed before combining, comparing, and removing quantities can be interpreted as arithmetic.

**Evidence.** C: K.CC.B.4–5 connects counting, cardinality, and producing sets. [S01](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/K/CC/) O: a cardinality/equinumerosity study used Give-N tasks with **51 monolingual English-speaking children**, aged roughly preschool rather than K–5, recruited around Boston; relevant methods were inspected. The population was not a representative school-entry sample. [S13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3830647/) I: early numerical games have intervention evidence, but that does not isolate this cardinality barrier. No direct efficacy evidence was located for this dispatch mechanic.

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** A request shows “6.” The learner selects counters from a larger stock into an unmarked receiving zone and chooses when to dispatch. Counters retain identity when rearranged. Repeated overshooting despite accurate one-to-one counting suggests a cardinal stopping problem; double-counting suggests another problem. After dispatch, the game rearranges the same counters and asks the learner to preserve or repair the shipment. Dispatching the exact constructed set is the game action, not picking a numeral.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: invite a check of the requested set without displaying its total. H2: mark each counter as counted and retain the last count word. H3: model a smaller set and explicitly link its final word to the whole set; return to a new target with the marks removed.

**Mastery and transfer.** Provisionally require unhinted construction of several novel small sets, including irregular arrangements, plus resistance to adding counters after mere rearrangement. Separate constructing a set from recognizing a pictured numeral. A later unassisted set task would be needed for retention.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Counter identities, one-to-one event history, requested cardinality, exact dispatch validation, and arrangement invariance.

**AI responsibility and honesty.** A suggested AI role would interpret an unexpected explanation about why counting stopped. However, this target learner may not produce reliable typed explanations, and the useful adaptation is already available from deterministic traces. No convincing indispensable AI responsibility is established.

**Safe fallback.** The entire loop works with pre-authored count-and-check prompts.

**Three-minute demo.** Show a synthetic reciter dispatch too many counters, count with support, then build a new exact set and leave it unchanged after rearrangement.

**Strongest reason to reject.** It is a credible learning problem but a weak AI product; adding a conversational character would likely be AI-washing.

### C02 — Interval Trail

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner recognizes numerals and counts ordered objects, but treats the starting marker as the first movement when asked to advance a number of units. The target is confusing positions with intervals, not poor general “number sense.”

**Why consequential.** That confusion can produce off-by-one errors in counting-on and later measurement.

**Evidence.** C: K.CC.A.2 and 1.OA.C.5 connect starting from a given number and counting-on. [S01](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/K/CC/), [S02](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/1/OA/) I: Ramani and Siegler's low-income U.S. preschool board-game study reports roughly **one hour** of play and gains remaining **nine weeks later (both abstract-level)**. This supports numerical board play, not this particular interval diagnostic or a three-minute dose. [S14](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ789398) O/limitation: research guidance discusses counting marks instead of intervals, particularly in measurement; extension to this learner's addition behavior is a product hypothesis. [S30](https://www.nuffieldfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Review-of-research-Griffiths-Teaching-fractions-and-decimals-to-children-aged-3-to-11.pdf)

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** Starting at 4, construct three unit bridges to a destination. A learner who places the first bridge “at 4” and stops at 6 exposes the distinction between a location and a movement. Equal-length segments connect numbered posts; the learner commits the route before any animation. Later routes begin away from zero and omit some labels.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: replay the first attempted movement. H2: contrast the starting post with the space to the next post. H3: model two unit movements, name their endpoints, then reset to another start.

**Mastery and transfer.** Build routes from unfamiliar starting points without counting the start; solve an equivalent addition with no trail, then measure a displaced strip from a nonzero ruler mark. Measurement is a transfer probe, not another content strand.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Unit spacing, path length, start/end relationship, and event-level off-by-one checks.

**AI responsibility and honesty.** Reading a nonstandard route explanation is possible, but “wrong start,” “wrong number of moves,” and “wrong direction” already support nearly all meaningful adaptation. There is no established AI advantage for the bounded task.

**Safe fallback.** A fixed replay/highlight/model ladder.

**Three-minute demo.** A misplaced first step visibly causes a short route; the learner rebuilds from a new start with fewer labels.

**Strongest reason to reject.** The best version is probably a small deterministic educational game; an AI layer adds little and number-path familiarity may dominate results.

### C03 — Ten-Frame Relay

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner can count-on and knows complements to ten, yet recounts everything for sums such as 8 + 7 or breaks an addend without conserving its total. The targeted barrier is coordinating decomposition with an unchanged sum.

**Why consequential.** Reliable decomposition can support flexible basic addition without making success depend on memorizing every combination independently.

**Evidence.** C: 1.OA.C.6 includes making-ten and related sum strategies. [S02](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/1/OA/) I: Baroody and colleagues randomly assigned **85 K–3 learners (abstract-level)** to use-10, subtraction, or drill conditions; transfer to unpracticed items was reported after extended computer-based instruction. Their use-10 treatment is related to, but not identical with, transferring counters to fill a frame. Country/site was not verified from the retrieved abstract; U.S. sponsorship alone does not establish participant geography. [S15](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1099308) O: the exact conservation error in this interface remains hypothetical; persistent counting is a strategy, not automatically a misconception.

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** An eight-filled frame and seven loose counters must be delivered as one full ten-frame plus leftovers. The learner chooses a subset to transfer, then commits a proposed total. Taking two without leaving five behind makes nonconservation visible. The construction must use exactly the original counters; no game points reward speed.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: ask what changed and what stayed the same. H2: highlight the two vacancies and track only the moved subset. H3: show one smaller decomposition with an explicit before/after sum, then remove tracking.

**Mastery and transfer.** Unhinted conserved decompositions with novel addends, followed by a symbolic sum and a verbal or constructed explanation of why moving counters did not add new ones. Accept another valid addition strategy; separately record whether decomposition was demonstrated.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Counter conservation, complements, decomposition identities, legal frame capacity, and exact sum.

**AI responsibility.** Respond to learner-authored decompositions and explanations, including valid alternatives such as compensation, and ask a probe tailored to an inconsistency between explanation and action. This is a modest semantic role; choosing which vacancy to highlight is deterministic.

**Safe fallback.** Validate all legal decompositions and use pre-authored conservation prompts; label unrecognized explanations unresolved.

**Three-minute demo.** Show a synthetic double-count of moved counters, a targeted conservation prompt, a repaired 8 + 7 construction, and an unscaffolded 9 + 6 task.

**Strongest reason to reject.** The AI can be removed with little damage, and imposing one preferred strategy could misclassify an already capable learner.

### C04 — Missing-Difference Bridge

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner can add small quantities and perform take-away subtraction, but cannot use a missing addend to determine a difference. Entry behavior: for 13 − 9, the learner insists on removing nine one by one and rejects 9 + ? = 13 as a representation of the same unknown.

**Why consequential.** This limits access to comparison situations and useful subtraction strategies. Slow take-away alone is not the target misconception; rejection of the inverse relationship is.

**Evidence.** C: 1.OA.B.4 explicitly connects subtraction and missing addends. [S02](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/1/OA/) I: the subtraction arm of S15 improved unpracticed differences after **twelve weeks of twice-weekly, thirty-minute sessions (abstract-level)**. It supports a conceptual intervention, not an instant shortcut lesson. [S15](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1099308) A later analysis discusses earlier instruction that failed to produce transfer; simply telling children to “think addition” is not sufficient. That later report reanalyzes the earlier research and must not count as an independent replication. [S16](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0885200619301012)

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** Align two discrete length bars of 9 and 13. Build the missing segment that makes the short bar reach the long one, then attach it to both 9 + ? = 13 and 13 − 9 = ?. A take-away tray is available so the learner can relate both constructions. The win condition is a bridge that exactly spans the difference, not choosing an operation.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: ask the learner to point to the unknown quantity. H2: align starts and expose the unmatched interval without counting it. H3: show a linked take-away and missing-part example, then rebuild the relationship on new quantities.

**Mastery and transfer.** Solve unseen close and non-close differences, connect each to an addition, and handle a comparison story without the bridge. Do not declare count-up universally superior; verify the relation even when take-away is efficient.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Bar lengths, gap, inverse identity, possible decompositions, and exact difference.

**AI responsibility.** Interpret a learner's explanation of what the missing segment means and respond to semantic confusion such as treating 9 as the answer because it was “taken away.” Ask for an action that distinguishes operation meaning from a counting slip. Correctness and selecting a close-number task need no AI.

**Safe fallback.** A paired representation and explicit pre-authored inverse explanation; unresolved language receives a “show the missing part” prompt.

**Three-minute demo.** A synthetic learner misidentifies the gap, receives a meaning-specific probe, connects the two equations, then solves a different comparison without visible bars.

**Strongest reason to reject.** The useful AI responsibility is limited, and a bridge could teach a visual trick without generalizing to subtraction language.

### C05 — Exchange Without Loss

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner counts ones and recognizes a ten-bundle but treats ten ones and one ten as different total quantities when units are exchanged. The target is value conservation under renaming, not every place-value or written-algorithm error.

**Why consequential.** Regrouping cannot be explained meaningfully if changing the unit is interpreted as changing the amount.

**Evidence.** C: 2.NBT.B.7–9 concerns composing/decomposing units and explaining strategies. [S03](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/2/NBT/) O: Mix and colleagues distinguish approximate multidigit understanding from explicit count-and-unit knowledge in a U.S. kindergarten-to-first-grade longitudinal study. This does not demonstrate how common the proposed exchange error is in Grade 2 or 3. [S17](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13724) I/against: a small kindergarten training comparison did not establish a general advantage of base-ten blocks; difficult symbolic items improved in the symbol-training condition. Blocks must be learned as representations. [S18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4460578/)

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** A delivery of 34 units must fit trays that allow at most four loose ones. The learner groups ten individual units into a transparent bundle, predicts whether the total changes, and can undo the exchange. A second request requires opening a bundle to provide seven ones. This is packing under an arithmetic constraint, not dragging a “carry” digit into its expected location.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: compare the inventory before and after. H2: open a bundle to show its ten constituents. H3: model a reversible exchange on a smaller amount and connect both unit-count names to the same quantity.

**Mastery and transfer.** Create two equivalent noncanonical names for a quantity, exchange in both directions, then explain a simple written regrouping step without blocks. Do not infer written addition mastery from packing alone.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Base-ten unit values, conservation, reversible exchange, inventory and capacity.

**AI responsibility.** Interpret a nonstandard unit explanation, distinguishing “one bundle means one item” from “one unit of ten,” then propose a contrasting inventory. With structured actions only, this largely collapses to a finite rule system.

**Safe fallback.** Open/close bundles with explicit unit labels and an authored contrast task.

**Three-minute demo.** A synthetic learner claims an exchange reduced the amount, opens the bundle to check, then renames a new quantity in two ways.

**Strongest reason to reject.** The exact learner barrier has less directly matched evidence here than the broad place-value literature; both the manipulative and AI could add complexity without improving transfer.

### C06 — Array Seam Workshop

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner can build equal rows and knows a few multiplication facts, but when splitting 6 × 7 into 6 × 5 and a remainder strip, treats the strip as “+2” rather than 6 × 2. The precise error is failing to distribute the unchanged dimension across both parts.

**Why consequential.** This obstructs derived multiplication facts and later area/partial-product reasoning.

**Evidence.** C: 3.OA.B.5 includes distributivity. [S04](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/3/OA/) The NCTM article by Kinzer and Stanford describes an area-model learning progression and related activities; it is not a controlled efficacy study or a prevalence estimate. [S19](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1020122) I: general manipulative evidence is adjacent, not evidence that this particular seam game repairs the stated error. No directly matched intervention on this error was verified.

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** A rectangular sheet must be built from available known-product panels. The learner cuts the seven-column width into five and two, labels each panel's quantity, and recombines them without gaps or overlap. Labeling the second panel “2” leaves most of its cells unaccounted for. Rotating the sheet is allowed; multiplication's commutativity must not be penalized because of row/column naming conventions.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: ask whether every square is accounted for. H2: highlight one two-square remainder in each of the six rows. H3: model a smaller split and connect both resulting rectangles to their products.

**Mastery and transfer.** Make a novel split in a differently oriented array, supply the matching distributive equation, then derive an unfamiliar product without visible cells. Do not equate accurate area counting with fluent fact recall.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Dimensions, area, tiling conservation, factor/product labels, and distributive identities.

**AI responsibility and honesty.** Potentially interpret a learner's unusual decomposition explanation and preserve valid alternatives. But all available cuts and identities are fully enumerable, and action-driven hints are deterministic. A strong AI case has not been established.

**Safe fallback.** Show unmatched cells and a validated panel-labeling example.

**Three-minute demo.** A “+2” panel label leaves ten squares unexplained; the learner relabels 6 × 2 and reuses the principle on a new sheet.

**Strongest reason to reject.** This is a promising mathematical puzzle with weak candidate-specific intervention evidence and an especially removable AI layer.

### C07 — Groups or Group Size?

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner can distribute objects equally, but interprets every division as a fixed number of groups, even when the divisor specifies objects per group. The barrier is confusing the role of the unknown.

**Why consequential.** The same arithmetic expression can answer different questions with different units. Computing a quotient without knowing what it counts limits word-problem understanding.

**Evidence.** C: 3.OA.A.2 explicitly includes both division interpretations. [S04](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/3/OA/) O: a fair-sharing study assessed partitive and quotitive division in **237 children aged five and six (abstract-level)** and examined individual differences. Its participant country was not verified from accessible material; it cannot establish local prevalence or a Grade 3 intervention effect. [S20](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475221000190) I: schema instruction is supported at the broader word-problem level, but this two-mode game has not been evaluated. [S31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6130842/)

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** Twelve counters must either fill three equal trays or make trays containing three counters each. The learner decides what to fix, what to construct, and what the quotient labels. Both rounds use 12 ÷ 3; one yields four counters per tray, the other four trays. A correct “4” is insufficient if the built configuration or unit is wrong.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: ask which quantity the request already fixes. H2: pin the known quantity visually and leave the unknown dimension adjustable. H3: enact a smaller example of both interpretations side by side, then hide the pin.

**Mastery and transfer.** Build both meanings for novel totals; correctly label the quotient's unit; solve a short request with reordered wording. A further probe asks the learner to construct a matching request rather than recognize a canned sentence.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Total conservation, equal group size, divisibility, role labels, and validation against the explicit task schema.

**AI responsibility.** Interpret the learner's mathematical paraphrase or self-authored request, then probe mismatches between the stated unknown and the constructed grouping. The system should accept meaningful wording variations instead of keyword-matching “each.” AI never determines the quotient.

**Safe fallback.** A visual instruction shows exactly which quantity is given; authored contrast prompts continue instruction while language remains unresolved.

**Three-minute demo.** With the same 12 ÷ 3, show the synthetic learner initially build three trays in both modes, receive a role-specific prompt, and produce the second configuration with correct units.

**Strongest reason to reject.** The apparent misconception may be language comprehension; if structured labels remove all ambiguity, the AI contribution becomes ordinary branching.

### C08 — Remainder Routing — wildcard

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner can calculate quotient and remainder, but always discards a remainder or always rounds up, regardless of what the question asks. Entry requires knowing that the remainder is smaller than the divisor; teaching that prerequisite simultaneously would broaden the loop.

**Why consequential.** A correct calculation can still lead to an impossible allocation. The gap between calculation and interpreting the requested quantity is directly visible.

**Evidence.** C: 4.OA.A.3 includes interpreting remainders. [S05](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/4/OA/) O: Pacheco-Muñoz and colleagues studied **50 fourth graders aged 9–10 at one private school in Puebla, Mexico**, with **seven follow-up interviews**. Body-inspected findings include correct division without adequate contextual interpretation. Convenience sampling, few problems, and local contexts prevent prevalence/generalization claims. [S21](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1382676.pdf) I: a U.S. lesson-study report offers instructional directions, not causal effect estimates. It also cautions to establish division fluency before contextual remainder interpretation. [S22](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1279152) An older frequently cited study involved middle-school students and is not treated as K–5 prevalence evidence. [S33](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED342647)

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** Seventeen indivisible counters, containers of capacity five, two explicit goals: “Store every counter using as few containers as possible,” versus “Make as many full five-counter packs as possible; leave leftovers in reserve.” The learner routes the counters and labels the requested answer: four containers versus three full packs. The same 17 = 3 × 5 + 2 supports both. Cutting counters is disallowed because indivisibility is a stated task property, not cultural intuition.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: point to the leftovers and ask whether the goal is satisfied. H2: highlight the exact condition that conflicts with the proposed allocation. H3: enact one small contrasting pair with unchanged numbers and a changed goal; then remove the highlighted condition.

**Mastery and transfer.** Solve new totals with both interpretations, explain what the answer counts, and handle unfamiliar wording without a keyword cue. Show a zero-remainder case so the learner does not simply add one every time. Fractional sharing is explicitly outside this loop.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Euclidean division, capacity, indivisibility, leftover accounting, and a typed task contract specifying whether the target is all-item storage or full packs.

**AI responsibility.** Respond to the learner's explanation of an allocation, identify which stated condition they ignored or misunderstood, and ask a specific counterfactual. It can help compare differently worded justifications; it cannot create unverified task semantics or decide what “fair” means.

**Safe fallback.** Keep a verified task contract visible and use authored “all stored?” or “all counted packs full?” prompts. On uncertainty, ask the learner to point to the unmet condition.

**Three-minute demo.** A synthetic learner computes correctly, leaves two counters unplaced under the all-stored goal, receives an explanation-specific prompt, and repairs the allocation. A changed-goal round then correctly leaves leftovers.

**Strongest reason to reject.** If success reduces to spotting two fixed goal labels, deterministic branching does the job. Richer language increases AI value but also comprehension and validation risk.

### C09 — Equal-Sign Repair — wildcard

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner can add the displayed numbers but treats “=” as an instruction to put an answer next, rather than a relation between equal quantities. The narrow entry behavior is an operational reading across nontraditional equations, confirmed by a constructed representation or explanation.

**Why consequential.** This undermines missing-number arithmetic and interpreting equivalent expressions, even when calculation is accurate.

**Evidence.** C: 1.OA.D.7–8 explicitly addresses equality and unknown positions; this can be a later intervention target without redefining the curriculum grade. [S02](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/1/OA/) O/I: McNeil et al.'s experiment with **90 children, mean age eight (abstract-level)** found better equivalence understanding after nontraditional than traditional arithmetic practice. [S23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21793817/) A subsequent classroom study tested a package including explanation, representation fading, and comparison against nontraditional practice alone, with positive transfer findings. Its small number of classrooms and bundled components limit causal attribution to any single feature. [S24](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000337) Against: a systematic review finds heterogeneous balance-model evidence, rather than proof that any balance interface teaches equality. [S25](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40594-019-0183-2)

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** Repair a paired quantity construction representing 6 + 5 = 7 + ?. The learner places units, groups equivalent parts, and chooses a replacement expression that preserves equality. An operational response of 11 makes the right quantity too large. The interface uses equal-unit trays linked to symbols, with an optional balance display whose arm lengths are fixed. It never permits physical torque tricks to substitute for equal quantities.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: ask what each side names. H2: show the two constructed quantities and allow matching groups across them. H3: demonstrate one equality with quantities only, connect it to symbols, then fade the quantities on a new equation.

**Mastery and transfer.** Unhinted equations with operations on either side, a nontraditional statement such as 12 = 8 + 4, and an operations-on-both-sides example without the display. Ask the learner to construct two different expressions for the same value. A correct missing numeral alone is insufficient.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Exact expression evaluation, equality, valid replacements, transparent quantity-to-symbol mapping, and predefined progression checks.

**AI responsibility.** Interpret an unexpected learner explanation against their edit history: for example, distinguish “the answer always comes after equals” from a simple addition error or a valid compensation strategy. It then asks a discriminating question or points to the specific claimed relationship. Recognizing the wrong numeral 11 alone requires no AI.

**Safe fallback.** Authored comparisons and show-both-sides prompts; mathematically valid constructions are accepted even if AI cannot interpret their explanation.

**Three-minute demo.** A synthetic operational explanation accompanies a wrong repair. The AI responds to that explanation, the learner constructs equal quantities, and then repairs an unfamiliar symbolic equation with the visual scaffold faded.

**Strongest reason to reject.** A balance toy may train visual equalization without changing the meaning of “=.” The AI role also disappears if explanations are restricted to a few known options.

### C10 — Fraction-Length Foundry

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner can identify numerator and denominator and partition a whole into equal pieces, but treats a larger denominator as making a same-numerator fraction larger. The specific target is coordinating number of parts with the size of each part.

**Why consequential.** Fraction ordering and sensible estimates become unreliable if separate integer components substitute for magnitude.

**Evidence.** C: 3.NF.A.1–3 connects equal partitions, magnitude, and number lines. [S06](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/3/NF/) O: an error analysis of at-risk U.S. fourth graders found multiple whole-number-based patterns; its participants were selected for low whole-number performance, so it is not a population prevalence sample. [S26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5018237/) I: brief experimenter-led studies support fraction magnitude comparisons following number-line instruction; a later design study distinguished thin linear displays from square displays. [S27](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27854466/), [S28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31374537/) Against: a German game trial found practiced estimation gains without transfer to comparison or fraction arithmetic in its younger subgroup. [S29](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35078086/)

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** From equal unit-length stock, manufacture a segment of 3/4 and one of 3/8. The learner chooses equal partitioning, takes three units of the chosen size, commits which will span farther, and assembles the segments on a shared track. The action combines quantity construction and a consequential prediction; it is not answer-option sorting.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: ask which whole was divided. H2: align a quarter-length and an eighth-length at the same origin. H3: model unit-fraction iteration and explicitly connect the three pieces to each fraction; then remove the partition overlay.

**Mastery and transfer.** Compare novel same-numerator pairs, then include a different-numerator contrast to detect overlearning “smaller denominator always wins.” Independently locate a fraction on a changed scale and explain a symbolic comparison without segments. These are proposed checks, not a claim to teach all fraction arithmetic.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Exact rational lengths, equal partitions, common reference whole, iteration, ordering, and scale changes.

**AI responsibility.** Interpret the learner's reason and distinguish denominator-only reasoning from counting marks, changing the unit, or misunderstanding the numerator. Generate a specific request to demonstrate the claimed reasoning on a validated contrast, rather than give generic harder fractions.

**Safe fallback.** Shared-whole alignment and a pre-authored unit-fraction contrast; if the cause is unclear, run another diagnostic without assigning a misconception label.

**Three-minute demo.** A synthetic learner predicts 3/8 will be longer than 3/4, constructs both, discusses the mismatch, then compares a new pair with fewer supports. The demo explicitly labels that as a provisional transfer check.

**Strongest reason to reject.** The most attractive visual mechanic resembles fraction games with demonstrated transfer failures. Active construction plus dialogue is a plausible improvement, not evidence that the problem is solved.

### C11 — Find the Whole

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner can name halves and thirds of a displayed shape but treats “one half” as a fixed physical amount, independent of the reference whole. The learner's difficulty must persist after understanding which object is being measured.

**Why consequential.** Fraction amounts become ambiguous when the unit changes, undermining comparison and later fraction operations.

**Evidence.** C: 3.NF.A.3.d explicitly conditions contextual fraction comparison on a common whole. [S06](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/3/NF/) Research-informed guidance discusses changing referent units and distinguishes equal measure from identical shape. It is a narrative synthesis with varied source populations, not a direct trial of this problem. [S30](https://www.nuffieldfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Review-of-research-Griffiths-Teaching-fractions-and-decimals-to-children-aged-3-to-11.pdf) O/I: this search did not verify a directly matched prevalence estimate or controlled intervention for reconstructing the whole. That absence lowers research readiness.

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** A segment is labeled “one third of the hidden whole.” The learner constructs a compatible whole by repeating that segment. A second mission keeps the physical segment but changes its label to “one half,” requiring a different whole. The goal is inverse reconstruction; it differs from C10's ordering of fractions relative to an already fixed whole.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: ask what is being called one whole. H2: show the relation between one part and the number of equal parts needed. H3: model a half-to-whole reconstruction, then offer a new part and denominator.

**Mastery and transfer.** Reconstruct wholes from different unit fractions, compare physical amounts when fraction labels match but wholes differ, and explain why numerical equality of fractions does not guarantee equal measured amounts of different objects. Do not teach that 1/2 as an abstract number changes value.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Unit identity, segment lengths, repeated-unit reconstruction, and separation of dimensionless fractions from contextual amounts.

**AI responsibility.** Clarify the referent in a learner's explanation such as “this is bigger,” asking whether “this” means the fraction, selected part, or whole. The semantic role is plausible, but it may be solvable with explicit pointing controls.

**Safe fallback.** Require selection of the named whole, show reference outlines, and use authored explanations.

**Three-minute demo.** A synthetic learner builds the same whole for half and third labels, identifies the changing relation, and constructs two compatible wholes from the same segment.

**Strongest reason to reject.** Direct candidate-specific evidence is thin, and language about “different halves” can accidentally create a new misconception about abstract fraction equality.

### C12 — Common-Unit Mixer

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner can add fractions with the same denominator and construct simple equivalent fractions, but adds numerator and denominator separately when denominators differ. The target is combining unlike-sized units as if they were one common unit.

**Why consequential.** This causes systematically wrong arithmetic and implausible results, not merely an inefficient calculation method.

**Evidence.** C: 5.NF.A.1–2 connects equivalent fractions, common denominators, and magnitude checks. [S07](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/5/NF/) O: Gabriel et al.'s study of **439 learners in Grades 4–6 across five schools in French-speaking Belgium** inspected conceptual and procedural components and documented componentwise arithmetic errors. Its local sequence matters: younger participants had less relevant instruction. [S32](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00715/full) I: representation-based instruction has general support, but this search did not isolate a controlled trial of the proposed re-cutting mechanic for this precise error.

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** Combine 1/2 and 1/3 units of material. A learner who proposes 2/5 must construct what those two fifths would actually supply; the resulting length is shorter than the original half. To complete the mix, re-cut both amounts into equal-sized pieces without changing their lengths, then join them. A common denominator need not be the least possible one.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: compare the proposed result with either positive input. H2: expose the unequal piece sizes being counted together. H3: demonstrate equivalent re-cutting on one amount and invite the learner to match the other.

**Mastery and transfer.** Add a novel pair with different denominators, justify unchanged value during re-cutting, and reject an implausible symbolic result without constructing every piece. Include a same-denominator problem to detect unnecessary denominator changes.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Rational arithmetic, same-whole constraint, exact equivalence, conservation during partition refinement, and acceptance of all valid common denominators.

**AI responsibility.** Interpret whether “two pieces” refers to a legitimate common unit or to incompatible original pieces; respond to the learner's proposed equivalence chain. If the game offers only fixed cuts, the useful adaptation is mostly deterministic.

**Safe fallback.** A validated common-unit demonstration and magnitude check; never let AI approve a false equivalence.

**Three-minute demo.** A synthetic 2/5 proposal visibly fails the quantity check; the learner re-cuts, assembles 5/6, and explains a new problem without the mixer.

**Strongest reason to reject.** Too many prerequisites may need repair before the one intended misconception can be isolated, and the AI is relatively replaceable.

### C13 — Decimal Zoom Track

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner recognizes tenths and hundredths but compares decimals as strings or whole numbers, for example treating 0.47 as greater than 0.8 because 47 exceeds 8. The narrow target is loss of unit value when comparing unequal digit lengths.

**Why consequential.** That behavior corrupts ordering and estimation and may survive apparently correct performance on easier decimal pairs.

**Evidence.** C: 5.NBT.A.3 requires comparisons based on place value; England also includes decimal ordering in its upper-primary programme. [S08](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/5/NBT/), [S09](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study) O: Steinle and Stacey followed **3,204 Australian students in Grades 4–10 across 9,862 tests (abstract-level)**; the body distinguishes longer-is-larger, shorter-is-larger, and apparently expert patterns. This is a historical, mixed-age cohort, not current K–5 prevalence. [S36](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED501133.pdf) I: teaching recommendations exist, but a directly matched controlled trial of zoom-and-compose remediation was not verified. [S37](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1096143)

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** Construct lengths for 0.47 and 0.8 from tenths and hundredths, then place them on a common 0–1 track. Zooming into one tenth changes display scale but not value. The learner must deliver a length inside an interval, then justify why changing 0.8 to 0.80 does or does not alter the delivered amount. No money representation is needed.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: ask what unit each digit counts. H2: expand one tenth into ten hundredths while keeping endpoints fixed. H3: model an equivalent naming and comparison, then remove the place grid.

**Mastery and transfer.** Unhinted unequal-length comparisons in both directions, a trailing-zero equality, and a symbolic comparison without the zoom tool. Include pairs where a superficial shorter-is-larger correction fails. Compare with a whole-number pair to detect overgeneralization.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Exact decimal-as-rational values, scale invariance, place-value decomposition, interval bounds, and equality of trailing-zero forms.

**AI responsibility.** Interpret a learner's self-authored comparison rule and construct a verified counterexample to that rule, distinguishing whole-number comparison from an overcorrected “fewer digits wins.” Rule interpretation may benefit from AI; generating standard distractor pairs does not.

**Safe fallback.** Authored diagnostic pair families and an explicit unit-conversion display. Unknown explanations trigger more evidence rather than a confident diagnosis.

**Three-minute demo.** A synthetic whole-number explanation produces the wrong ordering; zoomed construction exposes the unit mismatch; a new comparison and 0.8 = 0.80 test whether the learner merely adopted a new superficial rule.

**Strongest reason to reject.** Decimal error taxonomies already support strong deterministic adaptation, while intervention evidence for the novel mechanic remains weak.

### C14 — Shrink by Multiplying — wildcard

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner interprets basic fractions as amounts and understands whole-number multiplication, but predicts that multiplying any positive quantity always increases it. Limit the candidate to positive starting quantities and positive multipliers; zero, negatives, and general fraction division are not additional objectives.

**Why consequential.** The overgeneralization conflicts with multiplication as scaling and makes fraction products appear inherently impossible.

**Evidence.** C: 5.NF.B.5 explicitly includes scaling above and below one. [S07](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/5/NF/) Research-to-practice literature describes the misconception but is not a controlled primary-grade trial. [S34a](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ463197) A refutational-text intervention involved **87 sixth graders (abstract-level)**, not the requested K–5 population. Its abstract supports only a tentative adjacent intervention rationale; participant country was not confirmed. [S34b](https://pasithee.library.upatras.gr/ejupUNESCOchair/article/view/3210/0) An existing digital-game study concerns seventh graders, so it cannot validate a fifth-grade product. [S34c](https://www.utupub.fi/items/0025ab50-58b2-4c33-887f-17622ba2ab37)

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** A length of six units must be transformed to fit a three-unit slot. The learner chooses and constructs a multiplier using a partitioned scale control, predicts the direction of change, then applies it. Multiplying by 1/2 visibly creates half the original length. The follow-up uses a multiplier of one and another above one.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: ask what “half of this length” would mean. H2: split the original into two equal parts and compare one part with the whole. H3: connect that construction explicitly to multiplication by 1/2; test another fraction without an overlay.

**Mastery and transfer.** Predict and justify change for a novel proper fraction, one, and an improper fraction; solve a symbolic example without the shrink control. A working slider alone is insufficient.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Exact scaling, domain restrictions, factor/product relationships, and comparison with the original.

**AI responsibility.** Interpret and challenge a learner-authored universal rule using a verified counterexample. The AI should respond to the actual stated rule rather than repeat “multiplication can shrink.” This is plausible but not validated with fifth-grade language.

**Safe fallback.** An authored three-case comparison around multiplier one.

**Three-minute demo.** The synthetic learner predicts growth, applies a half multiplier, revises the claim, and handles a new scaling case.

**Strongest reason to reject.** The directly retrieved intervention/game populations sit beyond K–5. Novelty and an attractive animation cannot compensate for that evidence mismatch.

### C15 — Copy or Add?

**Target learner and barrier.** A learner can add and build equal groups but interprets “three times as many” as “three more.” The target is distinguishing a repeated copy of a base quantity from a fixed additive increment, not broad proportional reasoning.

**Why consequential.** Knowing multiplication facts does not guarantee identifying multiplicative comparison; confusing these relations leads to consistently wrong models.

**Evidence.** C: 4.OA.A.1–2 explicitly distinguishes additive and multiplicative comparisons. [S05](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/4/OA/) I: an experiment in **two English primary schools (abstract-level)** compared multiplication instruction through correspondence with repeated addition and reported an advantage for correspondence on multiplicative reasoning. Participants were younger and had not received formal multiplication instruction, so this is adjacent support, not a direct remediation trial of “times” confusion. [S35](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0885201401000582) O/I: schema guidance treats comparison structures explicitly, but should not be mistaken for a prevalence study or proof of this game. [S31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6130842/)

**Revealing action, representation, and play.** A base strip contains four units. Build a new strip that is either three copies of that strip or three units longer. The learner must choose a copy operation or an extend operation and construct the result. Then the base becomes five while the instruction stays fixed, making a learned keyword-to-answer association insufficient.

**Three-level feedback.** H1: ask whether the “three” counts units or copies. H2: outline one whole copy of the base. H3: build a small pair of additive and multiplicative comparisons with the same numbers, then return to a changed base.

**Mastery and transfer.** Construct both relationships with new bases; interpret reordered wording; give an equation and identify what each factor counts. Include a visual-only request to separate mathematical structure from unfamiliar English.

**Deterministic responsibility.** Base quantity, copy count, added amount, operation semantics, and exact matching of constructed quantities to the task.

**AI responsibility.** Interpret learner paraphrases that conflate “more,” “times,” and copies; use the learner's own proposed relation in a new validated comparison. The distinction between language trouble and conceptual trouble remains provisional.

**Safe fallback.** A visual copy-versus-extend contrast with authored language, while keeping an unresolved explanation out of mastery evidence.

**Three-minute demo.** A synthetic learner builds seven for three times four, constructs copies after a specific probe, and handles a new base plus the additive contrast.

**Strongest reason to reject.** The narrow error may be mostly a language issue, and the directly matched intervention evidence is weaker than the curriculum alignment.

**End of candidate generation.** Fifteen distinct loops have now been described: two early-quantity loops; two basic addition/subtraction loops; one whole-number place-value loop; one distributive multiplication loop; two division loops; one equality loop; three fraction-structure/arithmetic loops; one decimal loop; one fractional-scaling loop; and one additive/multiplicative comparison loop. The wildcard directions are C08, C09, and C14. No ranking was assigned within those descriptions.

## Selection criteria — defined after generation, before scoring

The following tests do not reward grade, topic familiarity, similarity to the index, graphic spectacle, or the number of features.

### Hard screening tests

1. **Learner-problem test:** prerequisites and a repeatable observable barrier can identify one learner; errors can be checked against a non-mathematical alternative explanation.
2. **Research test:** there is empirical learner evidence relevant to elementary arithmetic plus a credible instructional rationale. A directly matched intervention improves confidence, but is not mandatory. Curriculum relevance alone is insufficient. Adult or post-elementary findings alone cannot establish the targeted K–5 problem.
3. **Mechanic test:** the learner's essential action constructs, changes, or tests the mathematical relation. Correct-option selection followed by unrelated action fails.
4. **AI test:** the proposed product assigns AI a bounded semantic responsibility that could change instruction in response to unexpected reasoning. A finite table of observable errors, fixed hints, or symbolic correctness checks does not qualify. Passing means a defensible responsibility to investigate, not demonstrated superiority over rules.
5. **Safety test:** the loop can be demonstrated with synthetic data; mathematical validity and progression remain deterministic; uncertain AI interpretations cannot become confident claims or unsafe instructions.
6. **Three-minute test:** one complete error–support–retry–transfer narrative can plausibly be shown without teaching several missing prerequisites or requiring additional platform features.

Because no product was implemented, mechanic, AI, safety, and demo assessments are **design feasibility judgments**. None is runtime certification.

### Scoring rubric for retained candidates

Scores range from 0 to 4. Anchors: 0 = absent; 1 = weak; 2 = plausible with substantial gaps; 3 = strong opportunity with material limitations; 4 = especially strong relative to this set. These are ordinal judgments, not statistical measurements.

| Criterion | Weight | What earns credit |
|---|---:|---|
| R — Research fit | 25% | Matched learner/construct evidence, credible intervention mechanism, and candid treatment of contradictory results. |
| L — Learner discrimination | 15% | Actions and probes can distinguish the intended barrier from competing causes. |
| M — Mathematics in play | 20% | The mathematical relation determines the essential action and consequence. |
| A — AI opportunity | 20% | Unexpected reasoning creates a meaningful, bounded adaptation responsibility beyond a rule table. |
| T — Transfer observability | 10% | A novel, unhinted task can distinguish conceptual change from interface practice. |
| D — Demo feasibility | 10% | The complete value can be shown with one learner and little setup in three minutes. |

Safety and minimum research/AI fit are gates, not compensable points. Formula: sum of each weight × score / 4. Decimal totals are arithmetic transparency, not precision about effectiveness or winning probability.

### 1. Equal-Sign Repair

**Why it leads under these criteria.** It combines a narrow error that can coexist with successful calculation, a mathematical repair action, relatively direct intervention evidence, and a clear transition from constructed quantities to unfamiliar symbols. It does not lead because it is suitable for any particular grade. The learner is whoever meets the prerequisite-and-behavior definition.

**Why it could beat every other finalist.** It has a stronger directly matched instructional basis than Remainder Routing or Decimal Zoom Track, a clearer symbolic transfer endpoint than a potentially representation-bound fraction game, and richer differences in expressed interpretation than a single missing-addend strategy. Its units remain small positive integers, keeping the demo explanation compact.

**What could make it lose.** Its entire AI advantage may evaporate under comparison with a well-designed deterministic equality tutor. The evidence concerns instructional packages, not an autonomous explanation interpreter. A balance interface can also reward equal-looking constructions without relational interpretation.

**AI value test before claiming differentiation.** Compare an authored equality tutor with an AI-assisted version using adult-authored synthetic cases that have the same arithmetic answer but different explanations: operational “answer next,” valid compensation, arithmetic slip, and uncertain referent. Have an adult reviewer judge whether the resulting probe targets the explanation without inventing a diagnosis. This tests software behavior only, not child learning. If a small rule set matches all meaningful responses, reject the AI claim.

**Provisional demo spine.** 0:00–0:25 learner and wrong repair; 0:25–1:05 learner explanation and distinct AI probe; 1:05–2:05 concrete repair linked to the equation; 2:05–2:40 unseen symbolic transfer; 2:40–3:00 disclose synthetic enactment, deterministic checking, and the absence of learning validation. The actual video must remain within three minutes; no build or timing has been verified here.

### 2. Fraction-Length Foundry — strongest challenger

**Why it could beat every other finalist.** It has the clearest physically visible reversal of an incorrect magnitude expectation and supports a construction-rich game without a story-heavy setup. Compared with equality, it may yield more engaging repeated choices about partitioning and assembling. It has more directly relevant intervention literature than remainders or decimals, and more visibly different reasoning paths than a missing-difference bridge.

**Direct challenge to number one.** If learners can manipulate partitions and communicate their reasoning with much less explanation than equality requires, this could deliver the stronger independent play experience. That is an untested usability hypothesis.

**What could make it lose.** The negative transfer trial is a serious warning, not a footnote. A learner could become proficient at the apparatus while retaining a denominator rule or failing on changed scales. C10's transfer score is reduced for this reason.

**AI value test.** Use synthetic traces ending at the same wrong length for different reasons: denominator comparison, tick counting, wrong reference unit, and misread numerator. AI must ask a justified next question; it must not classify all four as whole-number bias. Compare this with a deterministic diagnostic tree. Better classification alone is not proof of better instruction.

**What would overturn the current order.** Strong adult usability evidence that the construction is understandable with fewer prompts, plus a robust unseen symbolic transfer design and demonstrable semantic adaptation beyond a compact rules baseline, would justify revisiting the comparison. Real learning evidence would still require a compliant later study.

### 3. Remainder Routing — strongest challenge on AI responsibility

**Why it could beat every other finalist.** It exposes “correct arithmetic, wrong solution” in a single visible state. Unlike magnitude or equality validation, responding to a learner's interpretation of a task condition naturally involves language and context. That gives it the strongest proposed AI responsibility among these finalists. It also demonstrates transfer by changing the goal while keeping the arithmetic constant.

**Direct challenge to number one.** An audience may understand an unplaced object and an unmet goal faster than an operational reading of “=.” If meaningful AI is weighted more heavily than direct intervention evidence, Remainder Routing can lead.

**What could make it lose.** Its empirical support is more local and less experimental. The simplified two-goal version might still be fully served by authored branching; richer language can create ambiguity that deterministic task validation cannot automatically resolve.

**AI value test.** Keep task semantics fixed and validated while varying synthetic explanations: ignoring leftovers, counting full packs, misunderstanding capacity, or giving a correct alternative representation. Require AI to identify an unmet condition or ask for clarification. It must accept answers to the actual goal, never infer unstated real-world rules.

**Demo discrimination.** Show the correct quotient before the conceptual error. Otherwise the product may look like ordinary division practice rather than an interpretation game.

### 4. Missing-Difference Bridge — strongest challenge on instructional simplicity

**Why it could beat every other finalist.** It requires small whole numbers and one inverse relationship. It may offer the lowest prerequisite burden among the finalists and has a relevant computer-based intervention precedent. It avoids the additional unit structure of fractions/decimals and the reading demands of contextual remainder goals.

**Direct challenge to number one.** If equality notation itself is an extra obstacle for the intended learner, matching an actual missing length may make the first meaningful action easier to understand. The original target remains subtraction-as-missing-addend, not a combined equality lesson.

**What could make it lose.** AI opportunity is weaker than for the other finalists. The broader study's long intervention does not justify an assumption that one bridge repair changes strategy knowledge. A visible gap may produce the right answer without inverse understanding.

**AI value test.** Synthetic learners who all produce the same segment should explain it as “the missing part,” “the number removed,” or an unsupported guess. AI should seek the appropriate demonstration rather than praise all correct lengths. If these distinctions are captured by a short authored probe sequence, choose that and acknowledge failure of the AI-product test.

**Demo discrimination.** Include a comparison without an already drawn gap and require the learner to connect it to an addition; otherwise the video shows spatial matching only.

### 5. Decimal Zoom Track — strongest challenge on diagnostic clarity

**Why it could beat every other finalist.** Its literature offers distinguishable and persistent error patterns; the exact decimal system makes counterexamples and visual checking straightforward. An “apparently correct” shorter-is-larger rule can be exposed very quickly. It avoids open-ended story semantics while allowing AI to respond to a learner-authored rule.

**Direct challenge to number one.** If the product can make unit-preserving zoom intuitive, it may demonstrate precise diagnosis and changed reasoning with fewer competing symbolic-format issues than equality.

**What could make it lose.** Those well-known error patterns make a strong deterministic competitor easy to build. The research does not establish that a zoom interaction produces lasting change, and some learners will need tenths/hundredths instruction before the target comparison barrier is isolatable.

**AI value test.** Use synthetic rules such as “more digits means more,” “fewer digits means more,” and a valid place-value explanation expressed unusually. AI must identify a useful verified contrast and refrain from correcting the valid explanation. If it simply selects a familiar error category, that alone is insufficient differentiation.

**Demo discrimination.** Include trailing-zero equality and a pair that defeats the opposite superficial rule; one corrected 0.47-versus-0.8 comparison proves little.

## Sensitivity and reasons not to treat this as a decision

The first two totals differ by only one ordinal point on transfer observability. That is not a robust empirical margin. If its transfer design is judged as observable as equality's, Fraction-Length Foundry ties for first.

If the weights move fifteen percentage points from research fit to AI opportunity, while holding other weights constant, Remainder Routing scores 95 and Equal-Sign Repair 91.25. This arithmetic illustrates a value judgment; it is not a better estimate of learning. It also shows why a final project choice needs an explicit priority discussion rather than hidden enthusiasm for a topic.

If all language/explanation inputs are replaced by a handful of structured choices, much of the retained candidates' AI opportunity disappears. Under that constraint, there may be **no defensible AI-product winner**, even though several good math games remain.

At least four serious alternatives challenge the leader on different grounds: C10 on mathematical play and magnitude instruction, C08 on semantic AI responsibility, C04 on prerequisite burden and instructional simplicity, and C13 on distinguishing superficially successful rules. None is a cosmetic variation of C09.

## Findings, hypotheses, and unresolved questions

**Verified findings from retrieved sources**

- Named curricula include all the arithmetic relationships proposed here; grade placement differs between the U.S. and England.
- Studies document a separation between successful procedures and some forms of conceptual understanding, including equality, fraction magnitude, and remainder interpretation.
- Particular nontraditional-equality and arithmetic-reasoning interventions improved outcomes under their own conditions.
- Fraction number-line intervention results are not uniformly transferable; a directly relevant game trial reports failed transfer.
- Manipulative and balance-model research contains important qualifications. Visual representation is not itself proof of conceptual learning.
- No retrieved study validates these proposed AI responsibilities, this three-minute learning dose, or these game designs.

**Product inferences**

- Construction, repair, allocation, and transformation can expose more reasoning than answer selection alone.
- Requiring a prediction before automatic feedback and later removing the representation could help distinguish learning from interface following.
- Unexpected explanations may provide a meaningful AI responsibility when coupled with deterministic math and bounded probes.
- Equality currently has the strongest overall fit under the stated priorities; this is a report-level comparison, not accepted scope.

**Hypotheses still needing evidence**

- The target learner can use the interface independently and communicate enough mathematical intent without burdensome typing.
- Explanation-specific support changes the next meaningful learning action.
- The AI can outperform a compact authored diagnostic tree on cases important to learning.
- A novel unhinted construction predicts later symbolic transfer.
- Any candidate remains understandable and interesting over repeated play.

**Unresolved questions that could reverse the ranking**

1. What prerequisite profile is actually reachable in a later compliant evaluation? No children or real learner data were available in this lane.
2. Can the proposed interaction capture reasoning without imposing reading, typing, or motor demands that obscure the mathematical barrier?
3. Which AI interpretations can be constrained sufficiently that faulty semantic feedback never overrides deterministic truth?
4. How well does the strongest deterministic baseline perform? No candidate earns differentiation merely because it invokes a model.
5. Can a later transfer task avoid reusing the same visual cue or trained number pattern?
6. What persists after a delay? A demo cannot answer this.
7. What changes for a specific language and curriculum? No Indian, multilingual, or globally representative learner validation was established.
8. What runtime, cost, latency, availability, and model/asset permissions would a concrete build require? No provider or dependency has been selected.
9. Are adult reviewers able to distinguish a plausible synthetic trace from a contrived demonstration? Synthetic testing can verify behavior, never child efficacy.

**No final project decision is made or recorded.** This report ends at candidate discovery and evidence-informed comparison. It does not initiate cross-review, implementation, child testing, or submission.

## Research limitations and stopping boundary

The major limitations are incomplete paper-index access, uneven full-text access, English-language search bias, several abstract-level findings, and weak directly matched intervention evidence for some candidates. No independent effect-size synthesis was attempted. Samples from the U.S., England, Australia, Belgium, Mexico, and Germany provide useful contrast but do not establish worldwide prevalence or transportability. Related papers from the same research programme are not counted as independent replications.

There is no child testing, no real student dataset, no implementation, no runtime safety validation, and no evidence that AI improves learning in these proposed loops. Those are open evidentiary needs, not claims concealed by the ranking. The report provides a provisional top five and a strongest challenger; it does not make an accepted product decision or proceed to cross-review.
